# The East Conservatory

The lock on the east conservatory gave on the third turn of the key, with a
dry scrape of rust that showered red dust over Owen Pellegrini's knuckles.
Inside, the air smelled of wet peat, cedar lath, and the sharp, crushed-mint
bite of bruised saxifrage. Three panes in the south gable were cracked and
mended with yellowed linen tape, and condensation hung in slow beads along
the iron ribs overhead.

Owen set his canvas satchel on the potting bench and opened the herbarium's
acquisition ledger. Dr. Beatriz Ramos had willed her living collection and
thirty-two field notebooks to the regional institute on the condition that an
archivist catalog every bench before a single pot was moved. The solicitors
had given him four days.

Dr. Beatriz Ramos was born in 1948 in Valparaíso, earned her doctorate in
alpine ecology at Zurich in 1974, spent twenty-two years teaching botany at
the university in Coimbra, and published four major monographs on high-altitude
sedges before retiring to this estate in 2006. She had been famous for her
1989 survey of the Rio Claro basin, a steep glacial valley forty miles east
of here that the national power authority had flooded in 1996 to build the
San Ignacio hydroelectric dam, submerging every documented colony of *Carex
clarina* under ninety feet of cold reservoir water. Since 1996, no botanist
had recorded a live specimen anywhere in the range, and the institute assumed
the species survived only as pressed sheets in the university vault.

He started at the northern bench, where the coldest drafts slipped under the
sill. Most of the clay pots held ordinary mountain cushion plants, each
marked with a narrow strip of stamped zinc wired to the rim. He read the
accession numbers aloud as he wrote them down, a habit from years in dim
basement stacks.

Near the far corner, half hidden behind a tray of dormant gentians, stood
three shallow terracotta pans filled with pale gray-green blades no taller
than his thumb. Their zinc tags were brighter than the rest, the edges still
sharp from tin snips.

Owen bent closer and tilted the first tag toward the gray light.

*C. clarina. Upper scree, Rio Claro. Oct 2009.*

He checked the second pan, then the third. *Nov 2011.* *Oct 2014.*

He stood up slowly, his notebook open against his palm. Rio Claro had been
underwater since 1996. No one had walked its upper scree in 2009, unless the
upper scree was not where the survey maps placed it, or unless Beatriz had
kept a location out of her published monograph on purpose.

Beneath the potting bench sat a tin biscuit box held shut with a rusted wire
twist. Inside were no seeds, only a folded topographic sheet from 1988, soft
as cloth along its creases, with a faint pencil ring drawn around a
nameless saddle three miles north of the reservoir's high-water mark. Beside
the ring, in Beatriz's cramped slant, someone had written a single line: *Do
not log until the timber lease expires.*

Outside, gravel crunched along the drive. Owen looked through the misted
glass and saw the estate solicitor's gray sedan pulling up beside the iron
gate, two days ahead of schedule, with a second man in a dark wool coat
stepping out from the passenger side carrying a surveyor's case.

Owen slid the folded map inside his acquisition ledger, closed the tin box,
and pushed it back into the shadow beneath the bench before the footsteps
reached the gravel walk.
