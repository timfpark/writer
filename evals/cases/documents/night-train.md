# Dining Car at One-Twenty

At twenty past one the northbound sleeper cleared the river bridge and
settled into its long, steady sway across the marshes. Nadia Koskinen sat in
the last booth of the dining car with an open orchestral score propped
against the sugar jar and a cup of tea that had gone dark as iodine.

She did not notice Bram Vermeer until his coat sleeve brushed the lampshade
overhead. He stopped beside the table, one hand braced against the mahogany
divider as the car leaned into a curve.

"Every other booth is chained off," he said. "The steward said the kitchen
stays open for coffee if someone shares."

"Sit down, Bram."

He slid onto the opposite bench and set a battered leather folio between them
without opening it. Five years had thinned his hair at the temples, though he
still tapped the side of his left index finger against the tabletop in quiet
sixteenths, a habit from sight-reading back at the conservatory.

The steward brought a white pot and a single cup, poured without asking, and
retreated behind the swinging brass door.

"Bruckner," Bram said, glancing upside down at her pencil markings. "You're
playing principal in Bergen now?"

"Second chair," Nadia said. "Substituted for principal on the winter tour."

"Good hall in Bergen. Dry in the balcony, though. You have to dig into the
C-string or it dies before the tenth row."

"I noticed."

For a minute only the coupling plates spoke, knocking softly underfoot. Out
the black window, the red lights of a freight siding slid past and vanished.

"I heard the quartet folded last spring," Nadia said. She kept her eyes on
the margin of the third movement, where she had marked a bow change she no
longer trusted.

"Martin took a teaching post in Graz," Bram said. "Once he went, there wasn't
much point pretending we were still the same group." He wrapped both hands
around the warm cup. "I still resent how you went to the jury behind our
backs during the Vienna finals, Nadia. It broke my trust in you completely,
and I have been angry about it for five years because I thought we were
loyal to each other above solo seats."

Nadia turned a page slowly, smoothing the crease with her thumb. "You knew
the fellowship only took one cellist," she said. "And you knew I'd been
practicing that concerto since October while you told everyone you weren't
even applying."

"I changed my mind in January."

"You submitted your tape three days before the deadline and let me hear about
it from the accompanist."

Bram looked out at his own reflection in the dark glass. A conductor passed
through the aisle with a silver ticket punch hanging from his wrist, nodded
to both of them, and pushed through to the next carriage.

"Did you take the fellowship?" Bram asked.

"One term," Nadia said. "Then my wrist gave out in February and I spent six
months relearning how to hold a bow without flinching."

His tapping finger went still on the Formica. "Nobody told me."

"I didn't send postcards." She closed the score and slipped her pencil into
the spiral binding. "Your stop is before mine, isn't it?"

"Halmstad. Half an hour."

"Then drink your coffee while it's still hot," she said, and looked out into
the dark marshes as the train gathered speed.
