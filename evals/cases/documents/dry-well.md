# The Cistern Ledger

The brass weight at the end of Tariq Haddad's sounding tape struck water
forty-one feet down, with a flat slap that echoed twice off the limestone
vault before dying out. He pinched the steel ribbon against the lip of the
wellhead, tilted his headlamp toward the markings, and read it twice to be
sure the glare had not tricked him.

Four feet and three inches below the red winter line. It was only late
August.

He reeled the tape back into its leather drum, wiped the damp steel through
his thumb and forefinger, and wrote the figure into his waterproof ledger
with a blunt carpenter's pencil. Above the cistern hill, the orchard rows of
Oakhaven stepped down toward the creek bed, where the willows had already
started dropping yellow leaves a month early.

Maren Lind climbed the gravel track from the pump house before he had closed
the iron hatch. She wore her county vest over a faded work shirt and carried
two tin cups of iced barley tea.

"You're early," she said, handing him a cup. "We didn't expect the watershed
office until Thursday."

"My truck lost an alternator in Calder," Tariq said. "Figured I'd measure
north to south while the mechanic waits on a belt." He held the ledger open
against his hip so she could see the column of dates. "Four feet three below
the red line, Maren. Last August it was eleven inches above."

Maren took a slow sip from her cup and looked down the valley toward the
wooden pavilions going up in the common field. The harvest fair opened in
nine days. Merchants from four counties were already booking hay barns and
paddock space.

"We've had three dry weeks," she said. "The ridge springs always lag behind
the first September rain. If you post a red-band notice on the square board
tomorrow, the feed buyers will cut their bids in half before the tents are
even up."

"The board notice isn't optional," Tariq said. "If the cistern drops another
two feet before October, the upper pipes lose head and the school has to haul
water by tanker. People need time to stagger irrigation before that happens."

"People don't stagger," Maren said, her voice flat rather than loud. "Three
years ago, when the state posted a drought advisory over in Fenwick, two
orchard families emptied their holding tanks in one night to flood their
blocks before rationing hit, and the dairy on the south road packed up and
left by Thanksgiving. They didn't come back when the rain did."

Tariq capped his pencil and tucked the ledger into his map case. He knew
Fenwick. He had stood in the dust of its empty creamery six months later,
measuring a well that had recovered too late to keep anyone in town.

"Hold the posting ten days," Maren said. "Let the fair clear. I'll call the
six big irrigators myself tonight and cut their pumping hours quietly. You
can check the meters on Monday."

"If one of them ignores you and the lower valve sucks air on fair Sunday, my
signature is on the blank line," Tariq said.

"And if you nail a red notice to the post office door before the buyers
arrive, half the growers in this basin won't make their winter loan payments,
whether it rains in September or not."

Down in the common field, someone tested the fairground loudspeaker, a burst
of static followed by a tinny count to three. Tariq looked at the iron hatch
at his feet, then at the padlocked chalk board visible just past the church
roof.

"Give me the names of the six irrigators," he said. "I'll ride with you to
their pump houses tonight. If all six lock their bypasses by sundown, we'll
talk about Monday."
