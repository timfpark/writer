# Salt and Iron

## Chapter 1: The Weight of Salt

The sky over Inis Breac was the color of a bruised plum, swollen and heavy, threatening rain that would not fall until it had soaked every stone on the island. Nora Faherty stood by the edge of the boiling pan, her hands resting on the iron rim, feeling the vibration of the water beneath. It was not yet dawn. The light was thin, a gray smear against the horizon, but the fires in the peat bed below were already roaring, consuming the black turf that Colm had cut and dried during the summer.

Steam rose in thick, serpentine columns, curling around Nora’s face before dissipating into the chill air. She smelled of sulfur, wet wool, and the sharp, metallic tang of brine. This was the scent of her life, the perfume of survival. Her father had died leaving her the keys to this place, the deed to the land, and a debt to the mainland bank that never seemed to shrink. Now, at thirty-four, she was the master of the salt works, though the title felt like a shackle rather than a crown.

She watched the water. It needed to be at a precise temperature. Too hot, and the salt crystals would form too quickly, becoming gritty and impure. Too cool, and the evaporation would stall, wasting the fuel. Nora adjusted the damper on the flue with a long iron rod. The movement was automatic, practiced ten thousand times. Her knuckles were white, the skin roughened by years of handling hot metal and cold water.

Behind her, the island woke in fragments. A dog barked from the cluster of cottages down the hill, a single, sharp yip that was swallowed by the wind. Forty-one people. That was the number. Forty-one souls clinging to this scrap of rock off the northern coast, where the Atlantic churned with a violence that frightened even the old men. When Nora was a girl, there had been two hundred. Then the fishing failed. Then the mill at Skerry Head closed its doors when the grain imports became cheaper than what the islanders could grind. Now, the salt was the only thing that kept them tethered to the map.

Nora wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of gray ash across her brow. She checked the time on the wall clock in the boiling house. Six-fifteen. The supply boat, the *Mary Ellen*, should be visible by now. Peadar Kane was late. He was always late, blaming the tides or the engine, though Nora suspected he preferred the mainland pubs to the dreary routine of delivering sacks of flour and barrels of paraffin to a place that smelled of dead fish and damp earth.

She stepped out of the boiling house and into the courtyard. The wind hit her immediately, pushing against her chest, trying to peel her coat off her back. The sea was a churning mass of whitecaps, the waves breaking against the lower rocks with a rhythmic, percussive boom that shook the ground beneath her feet. She pulled her wool cap tighter and squinted toward the harbor.

Nothing. Only the gray expanse of water and the silhouette of the lighthouse on the far headland, a dark tooth against the sky.

Nora turned back toward the mill. The stone grain mill at Skerry Head stood abandoned, its great wooden wheel still and rotted, the roof collapsed inward. It was a monument to the island’s decline, a skeleton picked clean. She had walked past it a thousand times, but today the sight of it seemed heavier. If the salt failed, the mill would not save them. If the salt failed, Inis Breac would become a ghost island, like the others off the coast that had simply emptied out, leaving behind houses that crumbled and gardens that turned to weeds.

She heard the footsteps before she saw them. Soft, deliberate, on the wet gravel.

Colm appeared from the side of the cottage, his hands shoved into his pockets. He was twenty-six, lean and sharp-featured, with eyes that always seemed to be looking past the horizon, toward something she could not see. He wore a shirt that was too thin for the weather, and his hair was plastered to his forehead by the mist.

"Peadar’s late," Colm said. It was not a question.

"He’s coming," Nora said, not looking at him. "The tide is against him."

"The tide is always against him. And the engine is always coughing. And the weather is always too rough." Colm leaned against the stone wall of the boiling house. "He’s probably having a pint in Galway."

Nora turned to him, her expression unreadable. "He brings the coal. He brings the flour. He brings the letters. Without Peadar, we starve or freeze. Watch your tongue."

"I’m not afraid of him, Nora. I’m afraid of waiting for him." Colm kicked at a loose stone. "It’s too quiet. Too empty. You can hear the wind getting louder."

"Go check the pans," Nora said. "The salt is setting."

Colm hesitated, then pushed off the wall and walked toward the boiling house. He moved with a restless energy, like a dog tethered to a post. Nora watched him go, then looked back toward the harbor.

And then, she saw it. A dark speck on the horizon, growing slowly larger. The *Mary Ellen*.

Nora felt a knot loosen in her stomach. It was not relief, exactly, but the cessation of anxiety. The routine resumed. She walked down the slope toward the small harbor, where the boats were moored in the sheltered cove. The water here was calmer, but still restless. The *Mary Ellen* rounded the point, her diesel engine chugging a steady rhythm. She was a low, sturdy vessel, painted blue and white, though the paint was peeling in long strips.

Peadar Kane stood at the helm, his face obscured by a scarf and a flat cap. He did not wave. He never waved. He simply guided the boat into the slipway, the hull grinding against the concrete ramp with a sound like bones breaking.

Nora stood by the bollard, a coil of rope in her hand. She waited for the engine to cut. Silence returned, heavier than before, broken only by the slap of water against the hull.

Peadar jumped onto the dock, his boots heavy on the wood. He was a large man, broad-shouldered and red-faced, with a beard that seemed to have its own weather system. He did not greet Nora. He went straight to the hold.

"Coal’s light this month," he shouted over the wind. "And the paraffin’s short. Supply chain issues, they say. Or maybe they just don’t care about you lot anymore."

"Unload it," Nora said. "We’ll pay what we owe."

Peadar grunted and began to haul sacks. Colm appeared at the top of the steps, breathing hard from the climb. He looked at the boat, then at Nora.

"Who else is on board?" Colm asked.

Peadar paused, a sack of coal in his arms. He looked up, his eyes narrowing. "Just me. And the passenger."

Nora frowned. "Passenger?"

"The government sent someone," Peadar said, dropping the sack with a thud. "Surveyor. From Dublin."

Nora felt a cold prickle at the base of her neck. She looked at the deck. A figure was stepping over the gunwale. He was tall, dressed in a suit that was too fine for the island, though the trousers were already stained with mud from the harbor. He wore a hat that had been blown off and was now tucked under his arm. He looked young, perhaps thirty, with clean-shaven cheeks and glasses that reflected the gray light.

He landed on the dock with a grace that surprised Nora. He steadied himself, then looked at her. He did not smile. He did not frown. He simply observed, his eyes sweeping over the harbor, the boiling house, the abandoned mill, and finally, Nora.

"Ms. Faherty?" he asked. His voice was soft, accented with the vowels of the mainland.

"Who are you?" Nora asked. She did not offer her hand.

"Sean Breathnach," he said. "I’m here to assess the viability of the island’s infrastructure and population sustainability."

Nora stared at him. The words were dry, bureaucratic, but they carried a weight that crushed the air between them. *Viability. Sustainability.* They were the words used before a place was declared dead.

"You’re the surveyor," she said.

"I am," Sean said. He looked at Peadar. "Captain, can you bring my bags?"

Peadar ignored him. He looked at Nora. "He’s yours. I’ll be back on Thursday for the salt. Don’t be short."

Peadar turned and walked up the hill, leaving the two of them alone on the dock. The wind whipped around them, tearing at Sean’s coat. He shivered, but he did not move toward the shelter of the house. He stood there, looking at the boiling house.

"The pans are still fired?" he asked.

"They are," Nora said.

"And the mill?"

"Closed."

Sean nodded, as if this was information he needed to record mentally. He pulled a notebook from his pocket and began to write, the pencil moving quickly across the page.

"Why are you here?" Nora asked. She kept her voice flat. She did not want to give him anything. No anger, no fear, no hospitality. Just facts.

"The Department of Local Government," Sean said, not looking up. "We’re reviewing the subsidies for remote communities. The costs of maintaining services—health, education, transport—are becoming... disproportionate to the population."

"Forty-one people," Nora said. "We keep the school open. We keep the church open. We keep the salt works running."

Sean looked up. "Yes. I heard about the salt works. It’s unique. But salt can be imported. Cheaper from Portugal or Spain."

"Then why are you measuring it?" Nora gestured to the harbor, to the stone walls.

Sean closed the notebook. "I’m measuring the decay, Ms. Faherty. The erosion of the breakwater. The state of the roof on the schoolhouse. The condition of the roads." He paused. "And the morale."

Nora laughed, a short, harsh sound. "Morale isn’t something you measure with a tape measure, Mr. Breathnach."

"No," Sean agreed. "But it leaves traces."

He looked past her, toward the village. Smoke was rising from the chimneys of the cottages, thin and gray, disappearing into the low clouds. A figure was walking down the path toward them. It was Father Declan Ruane, the priest. He was old, his face a map of wrinkles and weathered skin, his black cassock flapping in the wind. He walked with a slight limp, his eyes squinting against the glare.

Father Ruane stopped when he saw Sean. He looked at the mainland suit, the notebook, the glasses. He did not speak immediately. He looked at Nora.

"He’s brought the dog with him?" Father Ruane asked.

"No," Nora said. "He’s the surveyor."

Father Ruane’s eyebrows raised. He looked at Sean. "Well, well. A surveyor. In November. On a Tuesday."

"Good morning, Father," Sean said politely.

"Morning," Father Ruane said. He did not sound friendly. "You’ll be staying with Aoife?"

Sean nodded. "Mr. Kane arranged it. She has a spare room."

"Aoife Mullan," Father Ruane said, tasting the name. "A widow. A teacher. She’ll take you in because she has no choice. The house is cold. The roof leaks. You’ll be miserable."

"I’m accustomed to discomfort," Sean said.

Father Ruane snorted. "No one is accustomed to discomfort. You just get used to the pain." He turned to Nora. "The coal is short?"

"Peadar says so."

"Then we’ll have to ration," Father Ruane said. "Tell Colm to bank the fires in the boiling house. We can’t waste heat on the pans if we’re freezing in the classrooms."

Nora looked at Sean. He was watching them, his notebook still open. He was writing again.

"I need to see the mill," Sean said. "And the headland."

"You’ll see them," Nora said. "When I have time."

"You don’t have time," Father Ruane said. "You have salt to boil. Leave the boy to me. I’ll show him the ruins."

Sean looked at Nora, waiting. She shrugged. "Go with the priest. But stay out of my way."

She turned and walked back toward the boiling house. Colm was waiting for her at the door.

"Who was that?" Colm asked.

"Government," Nora said. "Surveyor."

Colm’s face changed. The restlessness vanished, replaced by a sharp, predatory interest. He looked back down at the harbor, where Sean and Father Ruane were walking up the hill.

"Surveyor," Colm repeated. "Like for a road? Or a new pier?"

"Like for an evacuation," Nora said.

Colm went silent. He looked at the sky, then at the boiling house. "Evacuation," he whispered. "They’re going to leave us here to rot, aren’t they?"

"Go check the pans," Nora said, but her voice was softer now.

Colm walked into the boiling house. The heat hit them both, a wall of steam and brine. Nora watched the water, but her mind was no longer on the salt. She was thinking about the forty-one people. She was thinking about the letters from the bank. She was thinking about the empty beds in the cottages, and the weeds growing in the mill.

Sean Breathnach was here. He had brought the wind with him.

***

By midday, the *Mary Ellen* had unloaded her cargo. The coal was stacked in the yard, the flour in the pantry. Peadar was gone, his engine fading into the distance. The island settled into its routine, but the routine felt different now. Fragile.

Nora stood in the schoolhouse yard. Sean was there, talking to Aoife Mullan. Aoife was a small woman, dark-haired and quiet, with eyes that had seen too much sorrow and had decided to keep it inside. She wore a dress that was too thin, and a shawl wrapped tightly around her shoulders. She listened to Sean as he spoke, nodding occasionally, but her gaze was distant.

Nora watched from a distance. She felt a surge of protectiveness toward Aoife, though she knew it was irrational. Aoife was a grown woman, a teacher, a survivor. But Sean was from the mainland. He had clean shoes and a notebook and the power to decide whether their homes would remain homes or become ruins.

Aoife looked up and saw Nora. She smiled, a small, tight thing. Nora walked over.

"Mr. Breathnach," Nora said.

Sean turned. "Ms. Faherty. I was just explaining to Mrs. Mullan that I’ll need access to the island records. Births, deaths, tax payments."

"You can have them," Aoife said. "They’re in the schoolhouse cabinet. I’ll bring them to your room."

"Thank you," Sean said. He looked at Nora. "I’d like to see the salt works. The process. The output."

"It’s busy," Nora said.

"I can watch."

Nora looked at Aoife. Aoife shrugged, a gesture of helplessness.

"Fine," Nora said. "Come to the boiling house at three. Wear boots. It’s wet."

She turned and walked away, leaving them in the yard. She heard Sean say something, but the wind carried it away. She walked toward the boiling house, the image of Sean’s clean shoes on the muddy dock stuck in her mind. He was an intrusion. A variable that did not belong in the equation.

She entered the boiling house. The steam was thick, obscuring the windows. Colm was stirring the pans, his face red from the heat.

"He’s coming," Nora said.

Colm didn’t look up. "Who?"

"The surveyor. Three o’clock."

Colm stopped stirring. He wiped his hands on his apron. "Let him come. Let him see what we do. Let him see that we’re not just waiting to die."

"We’re not waiting to die," Nora said. "We’re working."

Colm looked at her. "Are we? Or are we just boiling water until the government decides we’re not worth the fuel?"

Nora had no answer. She picked up the iron rod and adjusted the flue again. The fire roared, hungry and relentless. Outside, the wind picked up, howling around the corners of the stone building, testing the seals, seeking entry. Nora tightened her grip on the rod. She would not let them in. Not yet.

## Chapter 2: The Geometry of Wind

The mist did not lift so much as it retreated, pulling back like a tide that had decided to leave only after being paid its due. Nora stood at the edge of the skerry, her boots sunk deep into the wet shingle, watching the grey horizon sharpen into the hard line of the sea. Behind her, the island of Inis Breac was waking up in fragments. First the smell of burning peat from the cottages in the lower cluster, then the rhythmic thud of the hammer against the anvil at the smithy, and finally, the creak of the schoolhouse door as Aoife Mullan began to sweep the steps.

Nora checked her watch. Seven-fifteen. Sean Breathnach was late, or perhaps he was simply early in a way she did not recognize. She adjusted the wool scarf around her neck, feeling the damp chill seep through the layers. The salt pans were already boiling, the steam rising in thick, white plumes that twisted in the updraft before vanishing. It was a good day for salt. The wind was steady from the northeast, stripping the moisture from the pans faster than the fuel could burn it away.

She heard the footsteps before she saw him. Sean Breathnach emerged from the path that wound down from Aoife’s house, a leather satchel slung over one shoulder and a tripod tucked under his arm. He looked too clean for the island. His trousers were pressed, his shoes polished, though the mud of the previous evening had dulled the shine at the toes. He carried a notebook in his free hand, the cover worn soft at the edges.

"Mr. Breathnach," Nora said, not turning to face him fully. She kept her eyes on the pans. "You’re walking too slow. The tide doesn’t wait for surveyors."

He stopped a few paces behind her, catching his breath. He was tall, with a stoop that suggested years of leaning over maps. "I was waiting for you to finish your inspection," he said. His voice was flat, lacking the lilt of the islanders. It was the voice of the mainland, precise and unbothered by the effort of being understood.

"I don’t need an inspection. I need results." Nora turned then, her face set in the mask of duty she wore like armor. "Where do we start?"

Sean looked out at the sea, then back at the boiling house. "The perimeter first. I need to map the coastline. Then the mill. Then the population distribution relative to the shelter points."

"You can’t map the wind," Nora said. "And you can’t survey hunger with a ruler."

"No," Sean agreed. He opened his notebook, uncapping a fountain pen. "But I can measure the distance to the nearest emergency relief center. Forty miles. And the average recovery time for a gale-damaged roof. Six weeks."

Nora felt a flare of irritation, hot and sudden. She pointed down the cliff path toward Skerry Head. "The mill is that way. If you’re going to measure the distance to safety, measure the distance to the stone that grinds our grain. It’s useless if the roof blows off."

Sean nodded, tucking the pen away. "Lead on."

They walked in silence, the path narrow and slick with morning dew. The island was a jagged tooth of rock and heather, barely two miles long and half as wide. At low tide, the causeway to the smaller islet of Eilean Dubh was visible, a bridge of dark stone. At high tide, it vanished, cutting off the grazing lands. Nora walked ahead, her stride long and efficient, navigating the loose rocks with the ease of someone who had learned the terrain before she could read.

Sean struggled to keep up. He was not clumsy, but he lacked the islander’s instinct for footing. He paused frequently to take readings, holding up his compass and squinting at the horizon.

"How many people live in the lower cluster?" he asked, jotting something down.

"Twelve households. Forty-one souls in total, including Father Ruane and myself," Nora said. She did not look back. "You have the census report. It’s the same as it was ten years ago, minus the deaths."

"Minus the births, too," Sean corrected gently.

"Births are rare when you have to boil seawater to drink it clean," Nora said. "And when the coal boat skips a month."

They reached the base of Skerry Head. The stone grain mill stood there, a squat, white-washed tower that looked absurdly domestic against the wild, black basalt of the headland. Its wooden sails were furled, the canvas wrapped tight against the wind. The mechanism inside was ancient, a system of gears and shafts that had been repaired with scrap metal and hope for generations. Every autumn since 1890 it had ground the island's oats, and it would grind them again this year.

Sean approached the mill, running a hand along the wall. He pulled a tape measure from his satchel and began to measure the height of the doorframe.

"Why is this here?" he asked. "The wind is stronger up here, but the access is dangerous. The path is exposed."

"Because the grain comes from the mainland," Nora said, leaning against a rock. "And the wind is the only thing that turns the wheel. If we moved it to the village, we’d need electricity. We don’t have electricity. We have peat, salt, and wind. That’s the economy."

Sean looked up from his notes. "That’s not an economy, Ms. Faherty. That’s a survival strategy. And it’s failing. I’ve spoken to Aoife. She says the children are thin. She says the lessons stop when the fog comes in because the children are too cold to sit still."

Nora’s jaw tightened. "Aoife talks too much."

"She’s worried," Sean said. "She’s also your friend. She told me you’ve been sleeping in the boiling house for three nights."

Nora didn’t answer. She looked out at the sea, where the supply boat, the *Peadar’s Pride*, was a distant speck on the horizon, heading back to the mainland with the week’s salt. Peadar Kane never lingered. He took the cargo and left the bills and the letters and the news.

"I sleep where the work is," Nora said finally.

Sean stepped closer to her, lowering his voice. "I’m not here to judge you, Nora. I’m here to document the reality. If the government is going to send more coal, or build a concrete seawall, or even just subsidize the salt price, they need to understand the fragility. They see an island. They need to see a community on the brink."

"And if they see a community on the brink," Nora said, her voice low and dangerous, "what happens next? Do they send help? Or do they send a notice to quit?"

Sean paused. He looked at the mill, then at the path leading down to the village. "They send a recommendation. My report goes to the Department of Local Government. They decide."

"And what do you think they’ll decide?"

Sean met her eyes. There was no malice in his gaze, only a tired resignation. "I think they look at the cost per head. And I think Inis Breac is expensive to keep alive."

Nora pushed off the rock. "Then measure quickly. The tide turns in two hours, and I have salt to skim before it becomes ash."

They moved on to the village. The houses were clustered tightly together, their stone walls thick and whitewashed, their roofs thatched with straw that had to be replaced every three years. The wind found every crack. Nora showed Sean the structural weaknesses she had noted over the years: the sagging lintel over the Mullan cottage, the cracked foundation of the schoolhouse, the rot in the window frames of the rectory.

Sean measured each defect. He photographed the cracked walls with a small camera, the flash popping in the gloom of the interiors. He asked about the heating. He asked about the water supply. He asked about the food storage.

In the kitchen of the largest cottage, the home of the old McGoverns, Sean sat at the table while Nora explained the process of curing the fish.

"The salt draws out the moisture," she said, rubbing coarse crystals into the flesh of a cod. "It preserves it. But if the pans aren’t hot enough, the salt doesn’t crystallize right. It stays in the brine. And if it stays in the brine, you don’t have salt. You have dirty water."

"And if you don’t have salt," Sean said, "you don’t have fish. And if you don’t have fish, and the boat doesn’t come, you don’t have meat."

"We have potatoes," Nora said. "And oats. And seaweed. And stubbornness."

Sean smiled, a faint, sad expression. "Stubbornness doesn’t appear in the budget columns."

They left the cottage and walked toward the church, a small stone building perched on a rise overlooking the harbor. Father Declan Ruane was outside, smoking a pipe, watching the gulls fight over scraps near the quay.

"Father," Nora said.

Father Ruane nodded, his eyes crinkling at the corners. "Nora. Mr. Breathnach. I hope you’re finding the island... manageable."

"We’re measuring the distances, Father," Sean said.

"Distances," the priest mused. "A funny thing to measure on a place where everyone knows everyone’s business. The distance to God, perhaps? Or the distance to sanity?"

"Neither is quantifiable," Sean said.

"Ah, but you’d be surprised," Ruane said. He looked at Nora. "You look tired, girl. Are you eating?"

"I’m working," Nora said.

"Work is a good excuse for not eating," Ruane said. "But the body keeps its own ledger."

Nora turned away, uncomfortable with the priest’s pity. "We’re going to the schoolhouse next. Aoife is there."

They found Aoife in the classroom, arranging desks in straight lines. The room was cold, the air stillness heavy with the smell of chalk dust and damp wool. Sean walked around the perimeter, checking the windows.

"The glazing is original," he said, tapping the pane. "Single pane. No double glazing."

"We don’t have the money for double glazing," Aoife said, her voice bright but brittle. "We have the money for coal. Sometimes."

Sean looked at her. "How many children attended last week?"

"Fourteen," Aoife said. "Of the twenty-one eligible."

"Why not all?"

Aoife looked at Nora. "Because some parents take their children to work. The salt harvest is early this year. The wind is right."

Sean wrote this down. "Child labor is illegal."

"It’s not labor," Nora said sharply. "It’s family duty. They help with the skimming. It’s warm in there. Better than the classroom."

Sean stopped writing. He looked at Nora, then at Aoife. "It’s precarious. If a child gets burned, or falls into the brine..."

"Then we teach them to be careful," Nora said. "We teach them to respect the fire. We don’t teach them to be afraid of it. Fear makes you clumsy."

Sean closed his notebook. "I’m not afraid. I’m observing."

"Observation is a luxury," Nora said. "We don’t have the time for it."

They walked back out into the wind. The sun had broken through the clouds, casting long, sharp shadows across the heather. The air felt colder now, the promise of rain in the distance.

They stopped at the edge of the cliff, overlooking the boiling house. The steam was rising higher now, a white pillar against the grey sky.

"Your brother," Sean said suddenly. "Colm. He was at the mill yesterday. He was looking at the gears."

Nora stiffened. "Colm fixes what he can."

"He looked like he was trying to take them apart," Sean said. "Or build something new."

"Colm is restless," Nora said. "He’s young. He sees the mainland on the horizon and thinks it’s closer than it is."

"Is it?"

Nora looked at Sean. For a moment, she saw him not as an enemy, but as a mirror. He was also trapped, in a way. He had come here to make a decision, but the decision was already made in the minds of men who had never felt the wind cut through their coats. He was a messenger bearing bad news, hoping that by delivering it gently, he would not be shot.

"The mainland is far," Nora said. "But so is the rest of the island. We are all in the same boat, Mr. Breathnach. You, me, Colm, Aoife. We’re all waiting to see if the boat comes back."

Sean looked down at his boots. "I hope it does."

"Don’t hope," Nora said. "Measure. And then write it down. If you write it down correctly, maybe they’ll listen. If you write it down wrong, we’ll all be ghosts."

She turned and walked back toward the boiling house, leaving Sean standing alone on the cliff, his notebook in his hand, the wind tearing at the pages.

***

That evening, the light failed early. Nora sat in the boiling house, the heat of the pans warming her face. The rhythm of the work was a comfort: the scrape of the skimmer, the throw of the brine, the stoke of the fire. The salt was forming in perfect, white crystals, glittering in the dim light of the oil lamp.

Colm came in an hour later. He smelled of peat smoke and sweat. He did not speak at first. He leaned against the wall, watching his sister work.

"You’ve been out all day," he said.

"With the surveyor," Nora said, not looking up.

"Sean Breathnach."

"Yes."

"He’s a fool," Colm said. "He thinks he can save us with numbers."

"He’s trying to get us coal," Nora said.

"He’s trying to get us out," Colm said. His voice was tight. "I saw him looking at the houses. He was counting them like cattle."

Nora stopped skimming. She turned to her brother. "He’s doing his job."

"And what’s his job? To tell us we don’t deserve to be here?"

Nora felt the anger rise in her chest, but she swallowed it down. She had learned to swallow things. Anger, grief, hunger. They all tasted the same if you let them sit too long.

"His job is to write a report," she said. "And my job is to make sure there’s salt left to sell when he writes it. Go home, Colm. Eat something."

Colm hesitated. He looked at the pans, then at the door. "I’m going to the pub. Peadar’s back."

"Peadar’s not back," Nora said. "The boat left this morning."

"He’ll be back tomorrow," Colm said. "I need to talk to him."

"About what?"

Colm didn’t answer. He walked to the door, his shoulders hunched against the cold. "About leaving," he said quietly.

The word hung in the air, heavy and final. Nora gripped the handle of her skimmer, her knuckles white.

"Don’t you dare," she said.

Colm looked at her, his eyes dark and defiant. "It’s already done, Nora. I’ve spoken to him."

The door slammed shut, leaving Nora alone with the fire and the salt. The steam rose around her, blinding and beautiful. She listened to the sound of his footsteps fading into the night, and for the first time, she wondered if the wind was blowing the island apart, grain by grain, salt by salt, until there was nothing left but the sea.

## Chapter 3: The Weight of Ash

The boiling house smelled of wet wool, sulfur, and the sharp, metallic tang of evaporating brine. It was a smell that had seeped into the floorboards, into the rafters, and into the pores of Nora Faherty’s skin. She stood before the largest of the iron pans, the one her father had installed before the war, watching the water churn. It was not boiling yet, but it was close. The heat radiating from the peat fire below was a physical weight, pressing against her shins, her knees, the small of her back.

She adjusted the damper with an iron rod, the tip glowing red at the end. The flame licked higher, hungry and bright. Outside, the wind battered the corrugated iron roof of the shed, a rhythmic, deafening clatter that had become the island’s heartbeat. Inside, the only sound was the hiss of steam and the occasional pop of coal shifting in the grate.

Nora wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving a smudge of grey soot across her brow. She checked the clock on the wall, a brass-faced thing that her father had wound every Sunday for twenty years. It was six in the evening. The tide was turning. The water in the pan would begin to crystallize within the hour. She needed to be ready to scoop, to drain, to stack the salt into the curing boxes before the night watch began.

The door banged open. A gust of cold air cut through the humidity, sending a swirl of steam into Nora’s face.

Colm stood in the doorway, his coat dripping with rain, his cap pulled low. He was shaking, not from cold, but from a tension that Nora recognized. It was the look of a man holding his breath, waiting to see if the ground would give way beneath him.

"Close the door," Nora said, her voice flat. "You’re losing the heat."

Colm didn’t move. He stared at the pan, at the white crystals beginning to form on the surface of the brine. "It’s too hot in here, Nora."

"It’s the work. You know that." She turned back to the damper. "Go home. Eat something. Ma’s got stew."

"I’m not going home."

Nora paused, the iron rod suspended in mid-air. She looked at her brother. At twenty-six, Colm had the same broad shoulders as their father, but where their father had been a statue of stone, Colm was water, shifting, restless, unable to hold a shape. His eyes were red-rimmed, the skin under them bruised with fatigue.

"What do you mean, you’re not going home?"

"I’ve been to the mainland," Colm said. He spoke quickly, as if saying it fast enough would make it less real, less binding. "In Galway. Last week. When Peadar took the grain shipment."

Nora felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach, sharper than the wind outside. "You went without telling me?"

"I couldn’t tell you. You’d have stopped me."

"Because it was your duty to be here."

"Duty." Colm spat the word. He stepped into the room, closing the door behind him. The silence rushed back in, heavier now. "Duty to what, Nora? To this island? To these rocks? There are forty-one of us left. Forty-one people on a rock in the middle of the Atlantic, boiling seawater so we can salt herring for a market that doesn’t want it anymore."

Nora turned fully toward him. The steam curled around her like a shroud. "We have work. The salt works provides for the families. The mill provides the flour. It is what we do."

"It’s what *you* do," Colm said. He walked to the edge of the pan, not looking at her, but at the fire. "You’ve made yourself the keeper of the flame. You’ve decided that if you keep the fires burning, the island won’t die. But it’s dying, Nora. Look around. The houses are empty. The children are gone. Father Ruane is the only one left who remembers when there were a hundred of us."

"And where would you go?" Nora asked. Her voice was steady, but her hands gripped the iron rod until her knuckles turned white. "Where would you go, Colm? Back to the mainland? To what? You have no trade. You have no money. You have your hands and your back, and here, those are worth something."

"I have an offer," Colm said. He finally looked at her. His eyes were bright with a desperate, terrifying hope. "A shipyard. Harland and Wolff. They’re taking on men for the new dry dock. It’s hard work, Nora. Real hard work. But it’s money. Real money. Not barter. Not salt. Not promises."

Nora felt the blood drain from her face. The shipyard. The name itself sounded like iron grinding on iron. It was the mainland. It was the world that had left them behind, the world of noise and smoke and choices that didn’t involve the tide.

"No," she said.

Colm laughed, a harsh, brittle sound. "You haven’t even let me finish."

"I don’t need to hear the rest. The answer is no."

"You can’t decide for me."

"I am the head of this family," Nora said. The words came out automatically, a script she had memorized since the day her father’s body was lowered into the churchyard. "Father is dead. Mother is in Galway, in that home she hates. You are my brother. I have a responsibility to keep this place going. If you leave, the mill stops. If the mill stops, we starve. If we starve, the government comes. You know what happens then."

Colm flinched. He knew. Everyone on the island knew. The threat of evacuation hung over Inis Breac like a second sky.

"That’s not fair," Colm said, his voice dropping. "You’re twisting this. You’re using fear to keep me here because you’re lonely. Because you’re scared of being alone."

"I am not lonely," Nora snapped. "I am busy. There is a difference."

"Busy doing what? Chasing ghosts?" Colm stepped closer, the heat from the pan making him sweat. "Look at us, Nora. We’re ghosts. We’re haunting our own lives. I want to live. I want to see a cinema. I want to drink in a pub where no one knows my name. I want to fall in love with someone who isn’t from here, someone who doesn’t have the salt in their blood."

Nora looked at him, really looked at him, and saw not the boy she had raised, but a stranger. A stranger who wanted to burn down her life to keep himself warm.

"You are twenty-six," she said quietly. "You are a child playing at being a man. You think the mainland is freedom. It is not. It is another kind of prison, with smaller cells. Here, you have land. You have family. You have purpose."

"Purpose," Colm mocked. "My purpose is to shovel coal until my lungs turn to stone? To watch the horizon for a boat that might never come? I can’t do it anymore, Nora. I can’t."

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a folded piece of paper. He held it out to her.

"This is the contract. Or the offer. Whatever you call it. I start in two weeks."

Nora did not take it. She stared at the paper, a small white square against the darkness of his coat. It looked like a surrender flag.

"Put it away," she said.

"Read it."

"Put it away, Colm."

He didn’t. He held it out, his arm trembling. "If you won’t read it, I’ll leave it on your table. I’m going to take it, Nora. You can’t stop me. You’re not God. You’re not Father."

"Don’t you dare speak his name in this house," Nora hissed. "Don’t you dare."

The air between them crackled. For a moment, Nora thought he would throw the paper into the fire. Instead, he folded it carefully, placed it back in his pocket, and stepped back.

"I’m leaving," he said. "Not tonight. But soon. I’ve told you. Now you know."

"Go to your room," Nora said. Her voice was ice. "Sleep on it. Maybe you’ll wake up and remember who you are."

Colm looked at her, a mixture of pity and anger in his eyes. "I remember exactly who I am. That’s the problem."

He turned and walked out. The door slammed, and the wind rushed in again, carrying the smell of rain and distant, wet earth. Nora stood alone in the boiling house, the heat pressing against her, the steam rising from the pan.

She looked at the salt crystals forming on the surface. They were beautiful, in a way. Perfect geometric shapes, born from chaos and heat. She reached for the scoop, a long-handled wooden tool with a fine mesh bottom. Her hands were shaking. She gripped the handle until the tremor stopped.

She began to scoop.

The routine was a mercy. The scoop went into the brine, lifting the white grains. She drained the water back into the pan, the liquid running through the mesh with a soft sigh. She poured the wet salt into the wooden crate. *Scoop. Drain. Pour. Scoop. Drain. Pour.*

Each movement was a brick in the wall she had built around herself. If she kept the rhythm, the world would not end. If she kept the fire fed, the island would not die. If she kept her brother in line, the family would not break.

But the rhythm was faltering. The fire in the grate was dying, the coals turning grey. She needed more peat. She walked to the stack in the corner, where the blocks of dried turf were piled high. They were heavy, dense things, smelling of earth and time. She picked up two blocks, the weight straining her arms.

As she turned back to the fire, she saw Colm’s shadow in the doorway.

He hadn’t left. He was standing there, watching her.

Nora froze. She did not look up. She knew he was there, feeling the heat of her anger, the heat of the fire.

"I’m not going to let you do this," she said to the fire. "I won’t."

"You can’t stop me," Colm said. His voice was softer now, devoid of the earlier rage. Just tired. Just sad. "You’re trying to hold back the tide with your hands, Nora. It doesn’t work. It never has."

Nora dropped the peat blocks into the grate. The fire flared up, hungry and bright. The shadows danced on the walls, grotesque and elongated.

"Go to bed, Colm," she said.

He lingered for a moment longer. Then she heard his footsteps receding, the creak of the floorboards, the distant slam of the front door.

Nora stood before the fire, her face bathed in the orange glow. She looked at her hands, blackened with soot, calloused from years of labor. They were her father’s hands. They were the island’s hands. And now, they were trembling again.

She picked up the iron rod. The tip was glowing red. She poked at the coals, arranging them, ensuring the heat would last through the night. The salt in the pan was ready. It needed to be drained, washed, and stacked.

She worked through the night. The wind howled outside, tearing at the roof, at the doors, at the very foundations of the house. But inside, the fire burned. The steam rose. The salt crystallized.

When the first grey light of dawn touched the window, Nora stood back, exhausted, her clothes stiff with dried brine. The crates were full. The pans were clean. The fire was low, but alive.

She walked out of the boiling house into the yard. The air was cold, biting. The sky was a bruised purple, the sea a churning black mass. She looked toward the village. The lights were coming on, one by one. Smoke rose from the chimneys, thin and fragile against the vastness of the sky.

She saw the schoolhouse. Aoife’s house. The light was on there, too. Aoife was up. Aoife was always up.

Nora looked toward the cliff edge, toward Skerry Head. Somewhere out there, Sean Breathnach was sleeping in Aoife’s spare room, measuring the world with his eyes, waiting for the moment to declare them obsolete.

And Colm. Colm was in his bed, or perhaps not. Perhaps he was walking the cliffs, looking toward the mainland, looking toward the life he could have.

Nora pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders. She felt the weight of the island on her back, heavy as wet wool, heavy as iron. She had refused to hear him. She had refused to see the cracks in the foundation. But the cracks were there. She could feel them under her feet.

She walked to the storehouse, locked the door, and took the key. She walked to the well, pumped water, and drank it in long, cold gulps. The taste of salt was in her mouth, permanent and inescapable.

She would keep the fire lit. She would keep the pans boiling. She would keep the island alive, even if she had to burn herself to do it.

But as she walked back toward the house, she heard a sound. Not the wind. Not the sea.

It was the sound of a suitcase zipper.

She stopped. The house was silent. The door was closed.

Nora stood in the yard, listening to the wind, waiting for the scream, waiting for the collapse. But there was only the wind, and the sea, and the slow, relentless turning of the tide.

## Chapter 4: The Cold Ledger

The wind had stopped screaming, but it hadn’t left. It lay heavy over Inis Breac, a wet wool blanket that soaked into the stone and the bone. For nineteen days, the supply boat had not come. The sea had turned the color of bruised slate, churning with a lethargic, swollen fury that swallowed the horizon.

Nora stood in the boiling house, her breath pluming in the air. The heat here was a physical weight, thick with the smell of brine and peat smoke, but it was dying. The pans, usually roiling with white crystals forming on the black iron, were sluggish. The fire beneath them had been reduced to a dull, orange glow, fed by the last scraps of coal and a dwindling reserve of dried peat bricks.

She checked the gauge. The temperature was dropping. If it fell below the critical point, the salt would not crystallize properly; it would remain slurry, useless mush. She needed fuel. She had enough for perhaps two more shifts, maybe three if she banked the fires low and let the boys work the rakes with their own body heat to keep the motion going.

But there was another demand.

Nora wiped her hands on her apron, leaving dark streaks across the faded linen, and stepped out into the yard. The silence of the island was louder than the wind. Forty-one people. That was the number written in the parish book. Forty-one souls clinging to the granite teeth of the island, waiting for a boat that wasn’t coming.

She walked toward the schoolhouse, her boots crunching on the frost-hardened ground. The stone grain mill at Skerry Head stood empty to the north, its sails furled and rotted by the salt air. The wind here was thinner, colder.

Aoife was waiting at the door of the schoolhouse, wrapped in a shawl that had seen better decades. She looked tired. The shadows under her eyes were deep purple bruises.

"He’s coughing," Aoife said, without preamble. "The little Breathnach boy. And the Mullan twins. The cold got into the walls. The damp is rising from the floor."

Nora didn’t look at her. She looked past Aoife, into the dark interior of the building. "I have no coal, Aoife."

"I know what you don’t have. I have what you *won’t* give." Aoife’s voice was tight, controlled, the voice of a woman holding back a scream. "The children are freezing. The roof leaks in three places. The school is the only place warm enough for them to sit down and learn their letters. You have the coal. You always have the coal."

"The coal is for the pans," Nora said, her voice flat. "If the pans go out, the salt stops. If the salt stops, we don’t eat next month. If we don’t eat, you don’t have a school."

"That is a cruel arithmetic, Nora."

"It’s the only one we have."

Father Ruane emerged from the shadow of the schoolhouse porch. He was small, bent like a wind-blown thorn, his cassock stained with mud and age. He looked between the two women, his eyes bright with a wry, exhausted intelligence.

"The arithmetic is flawed," the priest said softly. "You are both right, which means you are both wrong."

Nora crossed her arms. "Father, I don’t have time for riddles. The fire is dying."

"And the children are sick," Aoife added.

Ruane rubbed his chin. "Come inside. The wind is a liar. It tells you the cold is outside, but it is inside, in the heart. Let us sit."

They gathered in the schoolhouse kitchen. The room smelled of boiled potatoes and damp wool. A small fire burned in the grate, barely enough to warm a tea kettle. Ruane sat in the only chair that wasn’t broken, while Nora and Aoife stood or sat on overturned crates.

"The government man," Ruane said, looking at Nora. "Sean Breathnach. He is watching you. Not with malice, but with interest. He sees a woman who chooses between her people’s future and their present comfort."

"He sees a surveyor’s report," Nora snapped. "He sees numbers. I see faces."

"Exactly," Ruane said. "And what do you see when you look at the pans?"

Nora closed her eyes. She saw the black iron, the white crust, the rhythmic scraping of the rakes. She saw her father’s hands, red and cracked, guiding her through the process before the stroke took him. She saw the ledger. The debt to the mainland merchant. The promise to keep the island alive.

"I see survival," she said.

"Is survival worth the cost of a child’s lungs?" Ruane asked. "Or is the school worth the cost of your livelihood?"

Nora opened her eyes. "There is no balance. There is only choice."

Aoife leaned forward. "Nora. Look at me. I am not asking for luxury. I am asking for enough heat to keep the ink from freezing in the well. I am asking for the children to stop shivering so they can read the page. If you take the coal from the pans, you save the school today. If you don’t, you might save the island next year. But who is the island for, if not for the living?"

Nora looked at the fire in the grate. It was dying too.

"If I give you the coal," Nora said slowly, "the salt will be poor quality. The merchant will dock the price. We will have less money for the winter stores."

"We will have the children," Aoife said.

"And when the boat comes, and we have no salt to sell, and no money to buy food, what then?"

Ruane raised a hand. "Enough. There is a third option."

Nora looked at him. "What?"

"The mill," Ruane said. "The grain mill at Skerry Head. It is old, yes. The sails are gone. But the stone housing is intact. And the wind, though it hides today, is constant. If we can repair the internal mechanism, if we can catch the wind with canvas and rope instead of wood and iron, we can grind grain. We can barter grain for coal with Peadar Kane when the sea clears. Or we can use the grain to feed the livestock, which frees up the salt income for fuel."

Nora laughed, a short, harsh sound. "The mill has been broken for twenty years. My father tried to fix it. It’s a tomb, Father. Not a solution."

"It is a symbol," Ruane said gently. "And symbols are heavy. If you show Breathnach that the island has life beyond the salt pans, that you are adaptable, not just stubborn, he may see us differently. But more importantly, if you fail to fix it, you have tried. And Aoife will know that you fought for her children, even if you lost."

Nora stared at the priest. She hated his ambiguity. She hated his sermons wrapped in logic. But she looked at Aoife, who was staring at her own hands, trembling slightly from the cold.

"I need coal," Nora said. "For the school. Tonight."

Aoife looked up, hope flickering in her eyes.

"Half," Nora said. "Half the reserve. The rest stays with the pans. And tomorrow, I go to Skerry Head. With Colm. We will see if the tomb can be opened."

The next morning, the wind had returned, but it was a dry, biting cold. Nora and Colm walked the path to Skerry Head. The grass was brown and brittle, crunching underfoot. The sea was rough, whitecaps snapping like whips against the cliffs.

Colm was quiet. He had been silent since Nora had told him about the plan. He walked with his hands in his pockets, his shoulders hunched against the wind.

"You’re wasting time," he said, as they reached the ruins of the mill. The stone structure was squat and imposing, half-buried in the scrub. "We should be working the pans. If the salt is poor, we lose money. If we lose money, we lose everything."

"We are working," Nora said. She pulled a lantern from her bag. "We are working on the future."

"The future is on the mainland," Colm muttered. "You know it. Father knows it. Everyone knows it."

Nora stopped. She turned to him. "And what about your future? Is it on the shipyard? Is that where you think you belong?"

Colm didn’t answer. He looked out at the sea, his jaw tight.

"Colm," Nora said, her voice softer now. "If I give them the coal, I am betting the island on their health. If I fix this mill, I am betting it on our ingenuity. But if you leave, Colm, I have no one to help me. I have forty-one people who look at me like I am God, and I am just a woman with a ledger."

Colm finally looked at her. His eyes were dark, filled with a restlessness that had grown sharper over the last year. "You don’t need me to be God, Nora. You just need to let them go. Or let me go."

The wind gusted, tearing at their coats. Nora felt a pang of guilt, sharp and sudden. She was holding him here. She knew it. The salt pans were a cage, and she was the jailer, and he was the prisoner who kept picking at the bars.

"Help me," she said. "Just for today. Help me see if the gears can turn."

They entered the mill. The air inside was still and smelled of mildew and old dust. Sunlight streamed through the broken roof, illuminating dancing motes of dust. The main shaft was rusted, frozen in place. The stone floor was uneven.

Nora set the lantern down. "The drive shaft is seized. We need to grease it. We have oil, in the store."

"I’ll get it," Colm said. He turned and walked out into the wind.

Nora stayed in the silence of the mill. She ran her hand over the cold stone wall. It was solid. Real. Unlike the salt, which dissolved in water, the stone remained. But stone didn’t feed you. Stone didn’t keep you warm.

She thought of Sean Breathnach, lodging with Aoife. She thought of his notebook, the way he looked at the island not with pity, but with curiosity. He was waiting for her to fail. Or perhaps, he was waiting for her to succeed. She couldn’t tell.

When Colm returned with the can of oil, he didn’t speak. They worked together in the dim light. Nora used a wrench to loosen the bolts on the bearing housing, her muscles straining. Colm poured the oil, careful not to spill it on the floor. It was a clumsy, awkward dance, but it was a dance.

By noon, the shaft was free. It didn’t move, but it was no longer fused.

"It’s not enough," Colm said, wiping his hands on a rag. "We need sails. We need canvas. We have none."

"We have tarps," Nora said. "From the salt stores. They are heavy, but they will catch the wind."

"And the rigging?"

"We will use rope. From the fishing nets."

Colm looked at her, surprised. "That’s a lot of rope."

"We have a lot of nets that need mending anyway."

They left the mill and walked back to the village. The wind was stronger now, a howling presence that pushed them forward. When they reached the boiling house, the fire was low. The salt was forming, but slowly.

Nora went to the store and took out the tarps. They were stiff with age, smelling of fish and brine. She carried them to the mill with Colm following behind.

As they worked, cutting the tarps and tying them to the old wooden beams, Nora felt a strange sense of calm. It wasn’t hope, exactly. It was resignation, mixed with a stubborn refusal to stop.

When the sun began to set, the wind picked up. The tarps snapped and flapped, a violent, rhythmic sound. The shaft groaned.

"Push it!" Nora shouted over the wind.

Colm pushed the heavy wheel. Nora pulled the lever. The shaft turned. Slowly, agonizingly, it turned. The stone wheels inside the mill began to grind against each other.

"It works," Colm whispered.

Nora smiled. It was a small smile, barely there. "It’s not enough," she said. "But it’s something."

They walked back to the village as the first stars appeared. The schoolhouse was dark. Nora felt a knot of anxiety in her stomach. Had the coal been enough? Had Aoife’s children slept?

They reached the schoolhouse. The door was open. A warm light spilled out into the yard.

Aoife was sitting at the table, marking papers. The children were gone, but the room was warm. The smell of burning coal hung in the air.

Aoife looked up. Her face was pale, but her eyes were steady.

"Nora," she said.

Nora stepped inside. "How are they?"

"Better," Aoife said. "The fever broke in the night. They are sleeping."

Nora let out a breath she didn’t know she was holding. She looked at the fire. It was small, but it was there.

"Thank you," Aoife said.

Nora nodded. She couldn’t speak. The pride was a dangerous thing; it made you soft. She turned and walked out into the cold.

Colm was waiting by the gate. "Are you going back to the pans?" he asked.

"Yes," Nora said. "The fire needs tending."

"Will you tell Breathnach?"

Nora looked at the dark shape of the priest’s house, where Sean Breathnach was likely writing his notes. "No," she said. "Let him wonder. Let him see the smoke from the school. Let him see the fire from the mill. Let him decide for himself what we are."

She walked toward the boiling house, the wind tearing at her coat. The island was still here. For tonight, it was still here.

## Chapter 5: The Weight of Water

The storm had broken three days prior, but the island remained sealed in a grey, suffocating silence. The sea, usually a chaotic churn of whitecaps and grey troughs, lay flat and heavy, like poured lead. It was a stillness that felt more threatening than the wind, a held breath waiting for a verdict.

Nora woke before the light. The cold in the house was a physical thing, pressing against the glass of the window, seeping through the walls of the boiling house. She dressed in the dark, her fingers stiff, wrapping wool around her ankles before pulling on her boots. The salt pans waited. They always waited.

When she stepped out into the yard, the air tasted of brine and wet ash. The supply boat had not come. It had not come for twenty-two days. The coal stockpile in the shed was low, a dark, dusty heap that smelled of ancient forests and damp earth. She calculated the burn rate in her head, a grim arithmetic that had become second nature since her father’s death. If she kept the pans at a rolling boil, the coal would last a week. If she let them simmer, barely keeping the brine from freezing, they might stretch to ten. But ten days was not enough to guarantee a harvest, and without a harvest, there was no money for the next delivery.

She pushed open the heavy door of the boiling house. The heat inside was a wall of moisture and steam, thick enough to chew. The great iron pans sat like shallow lakes, their surfaces trembling under the heat from the peat fires below. The smell was sharp, metallic, and overwhelmingly saline.

A figure was already there, crouched by the firebox.

Sean Breathnach stood up as she entered, wiping his hands on a rag that was already stained black with soot. He looked tired. The isolation had stripped away the crispness of his mainland clothes; his shirt was untucked, his hair matted with sweat and steam. He had taken to sleeping in the barn behind Aoife’s house, and waking before dawn to help Nora. It was not a request she had made, nor one she had encouraged, but he had simply appeared, day after day, moving with a clumsy but earnest determination.

"Brine’s thickening," he said, nodding toward the pans. His accent was soft, educated, a contrast to the guttural vowels of the islanders. "The water line is dropping."

Nora said nothing. She moved past him, checking the flow from the cisterns. The pipes groaned, a low, tectonic sound in the stone walls. She adjusted a valve, her hand steady despite the ache in her knuckles.

"I checked the coal," Sean added. He didn’t ask permission to speak; he had learned that Nora did not engage in conversation, but she listened to facts. "There’s enough for two days at full heat. Maybe three if we bank the fires."

Nora turned, her face a mask of damp hair and flushed skin. "And the school?"

"Aoife said the children are coughing. The damp has gotten into the walls. They need heat."

"The pans need heat," Nora said, her voice flat. "Salt doesn’t care if the children are cold. Salt cares about fire."

Sean looked at the pans, then at her. There was no judgment in his eyes, only a weary recognition of the trap. "If you don’t harvest, the government won’t send money for coal. If you don’t have coal, the school freezes. If the school freezes, they leave. If they leave, the census drops below thirty. If it drops below thirty, the ferry stops running entirely."

Nora’s jaw tightened. He was reciting the logic of his report back to her, the very document that hung over the island like a guillotine. She hated him for his clarity. She hated him because he was right, and because his presence here, in the steam and the soot, made the abstraction of 'evacuation' painfully concrete. He was not a man in a suit in Dublin anymore; he was a man with blackened hands and a cough, standing in her boiling house.

"Move the logs," she said finally. "Don’t touch the valves."

He nodded and returned to the firebox. For hours, they worked in a rhythm that was neither conversation nor silence, but a shared labor. Nora skimming the impurities from the surface of the brine with a long-handled ladle; Sean stoking the fires, feeding the peat bricks with careful precision. The heat was brutal. It baked the skin and dried the eyes until they felt like sandpaper.

By mid-morning, the sun had not broken through the clouds, but the light in the boiling house had shifted from grey to a dull, bruised purple. Nora’s arms ached. She set the ladle down and walked to the heavy wooden door, pushing it open. The cold hit her like a slap, shocking the steam from her clothes. She leaned against the stone frame, breathing in the sharp, clean air of the outside world.

Sean followed her out a moment later. He carried a bucket of ash, dumping it into the wheelbarrow with a clatter that sounded too loud in the quiet yard. He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand, leaving a smear of soot across his brow.

"You should go in," Nora said. "You’re shivering."

"I’m fine." He didn’t move. He looked out over the yard, toward the cliff edge where the wind had carved the stone into smooth, bone-like shapes. "Do you remember the last time the boat came before this?"

Nora pulled her coat tighter. "Four weeks ago. Peadar brought mail. And sugar."

"And before that?"

"Six weeks."

Sean looked at her. "My report isn’t just about numbers, Nora. It’s about viability. A place isn’t just stone and salt. It’s people. It’s hope."

Nora laughed, a short, dry sound. "Hope doesn’t boil water, Breathnach."

"No," he agreed. "But despair does something worse. It freezes you in place."

He stepped closer, and she flinched, though she didn’t know why. It was not fear of him, exactly, but fear of the intimacy his proximity implied. He was an outsider, a man who would leave, and yet he was the only one who understood the weight she carried.

"I have to tell you something," he said. His voice was lower, stripped of its usual professional detachment.

Nora looked at him, her eyes narrowing. "Tell me what?"

"The draft is done."

She waited. The wind whistled through the cracks in the door frame.

"I’ve spent the last month walking this island. Measuring the tides, checking the structural integrity of the mill, counting the mouths to feed." He paused, looking down at his boots. "The island is dying, Nora. Not just the population. The infrastructure. The roads are gone. The school is a hazard. The salt works... you’re working yourself to death to keep a business alive that has no market on the mainland anymore. The shipping costs eat the profit."

Nora felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach. She looked away, toward the sea. The water was still black and motionless.

"You’re going to recommend evacuation," she said. It wasn’t a question.

Sean didn’t answer immediately. The silence stretched, filled only by the distant crash of waves against the lower rocks.

"I’m going to recommend that the state withdraw support for the salt works," he said finally. "And that the island be designated for voluntary relocation. Assistance will be offered. Housing in Galway. Jobs in the shipyards."

"Colm," Nora whispered.

"Yes."

The name hung in the air. Colm, with his restless eyes and his talk of the mainland, of money, of a life that wasn’t bound to the tide. If the state pulled the plug, Colm would have his excuse. He would leave, and Nora would be left alone with the pans and the silence.

"You’re killing us," Nora said, her voice trembling with a rage she couldn’t contain. "You sit here, eating our food, sleeping under our roof, and you’re writing the death certificate for this place."

Sean looked at her, and for the first time, she saw shame in his face. "I’m trying to save you from a slower death. A death by inches. Freezing in the winter, starving in the summer. Is that what you want? To be the last woman on a rock, boiling seawater for a world that has forgotten you exist?"

"I want to survive," she said. "That’s all. Survival isn’t a luxury. It’s a job."

"Is it?" Sean asked. "Or is it just a habit?"

He turned and walked back into the boiling house. Nora remained in the yard, watching the steam curl from the chimney like a grey ghost. She felt a sudden, violent urge to throw him into the sea, to let the cold take him, to end the judgment he represented. But she stayed. She went inside.

The rest of the day passed in a blur of labor and resentment. Sean worked harder than before, as if trying to atone for his words. He lifted heavy sacks of coal that Nora usually struggled with. He cleaned the pans with a vigor that bordered on frantic. When they stopped for the midday meal—bread, butter, and a tin of sardines—they sat at the rough-hewn table in the corner of the boiling house, the steam still rising from the floor vents.

Aoife arrived at two o’clock. She had brought a pot of soup, a gesture that surprised them both. She wore a thick wool shawl, her hair covered by a scarf. She looked at Sean, then at Nora, her expression unreadable.

"The children are coughing," she said simply. She placed the pot on the stove and ladled three bowls.

Nora ate in silence. The soup was hot, salty, rich with bone broth. It warmed her from the inside out. She watched Sean eating. He ate quickly, efficiently, like a man who had learned not to waste time.

"Father Ruane wants to see you," Aoife said to Nora. "He says the storm has broken the spirit of the village. He wants to hold a meeting. Tonight."

Nora looked at Sean. "Tell him no."

"He’s the priest," Aoife said. "He’ll come to the boiling house if he has to."

"I have work to do."

"Nora," Aoife said, her voice gentle but firm. "He’s worried about Colm. He’s worried about you. And he’s worried about Mr. Breathnach."

Sean looked up from his bowl. "I don’t need protection, Mrs. Mullan."

"You need to be seen," Aoife said. "Not as a judge. As a man."

Nora slammed her spoon down. "I don’t care what he is. I care about the pans. If the brine freezes tonight, we lose two weeks’ work."

Sean stood up. "I’ll take the night shift. You can go to the meeting."

Nora stared at him. "You don’t know how to manage the fires. You’ll let the salt crust over."

"Teach me," Sean said. "Show me what to watch for. What the color means. When to add water."

Nora looked at Aoife, then at Sean. The idea of leaving him alone with the pans was insane. It was inviting disaster. But the idea of staying, of being trapped in this heat while the island debated its fate, was worse.

She stood up. "Come here."

For the next hour, she instructed him. She showed him how to read the shimmer of the brine, how to listen to the crackle of the peat, how to feel the heat radiating from the iron. She spoke quickly, aggressively, as if trying to beat the knowledge into him before the storm returned. Sean listened, nodding, his face serious.

When she was done, she handed him the ladle. "Don’t let it boil dry. Don’t let it go cold. If you’re unsure, wake me."

"I won’t wake you," Sean said. "I’ll manage."

Nora pulled on her coat. She didn’t look back as she walked out of the boiling house. The cold air hit her again, but this time, it felt less like a punishment and more like a release.

The walk to the schoolhouse was short, but the wind was biting. The sky was dark, the stars hidden by a thick blanket of cloud. She could see the lights of the schoolhouse, warm and yellow against the gloom. She could hear voices inside, low and murmuring.

She hesitated at the door. Her hand hovered over the latch. She was tired. Her bones ached. She wanted to go home, to collapse into her bed, to let the island decide its own fate without her.

But she thought of the pans. She thought of Sean, alone in the steam, trying to learn a language of fire and salt that had been spoken in her family for three generations. She thought of Colm, waiting in the shadows, ready to run.

Nora pushed the door open. The heat of the room washed over her, smelling of wet wool and tobacco. Forty-one people were there. Father Ruane stood at the front, his hands clasped behind his back. He looked old, smaller than usual, but his eyes were sharp.

Nora stepped inside. The room fell silent. She walked to the front, not to speak, but to stand. To witness. To be the anchor.

Sean’s report was not yet delivered. But the verdict was already in the air, hanging heavy and wet, like the rain that would eventually fall. She looked at the faces of her neighbors, the weathered skin, the tired eyes. They were waiting for her to lead them. They were waiting for her to say it was worth it.

She didn’t speak. She just stood there, feeling the weight of the salt in her blood, and waited for the storm to pass.

## Chapter 6: The Fiddle and the Flame

The wind had dropped for the first time in a week, leaving the island suspended in a brittle, silent cold. Inside the schoolhouse, the air was thick with the smell of peat smoke, damp wool, and roasting potatoes. Forty-one people had lived on Inis Breac when the year began; now, with the winter’s toll and the slow drift of families to the mainland, there were thirty-two. They crowded into the single room where Aoife taught the children their letters, the benches scraped together to form walls around the central fire.

Nora stood by the door, her back against the rough-hewn frame. She had come early to help set the tables, but the noise and the heat made her feel unmoored. Her hands, still stained with the grey residue of the salt pans, wiped uselessly against her apron. She watched the men shed their coats, revealing the stiff shirts beneath, and the women adjust their shawls, trying to look respectable despite the exhaustion etched into their faces.

Father Ruane arrived last, shaking snow from his hat. He looked smaller than usual, his old bones creaking as he settled onto a bench near the hearth. He caught Nora’s eye and gave a faint, wry nod, as if acknowledging the absurdity of celebrating the birth of a savior on a rock that seemed intent on drowning them.

Aoife moved through the room like a current, directing the placement of plates and mugs. She wore a dress of deep green wool, her dark hair pinned back severely. She looked tired, but her eyes were bright, scanning the room for any need. When she passed Nora, she paused.

“You should sit,” Aoife said softly.

“I’m fine,” Nora replied. The lie tasted like ash.

Sean Breathnach stood near the window, looking out at the black sea. He had been stranded on the island for two weeks now, his surveyor’s gear gathering dust in Aoife’s spare room. He had helped Nora at the pans that morning, carrying sacks of coal with a strength that surprised her, though he complained of his back within the hour. He did not know what to do with himself when he wasn’t working or measuring. He stood with his hands in his pockets, looking at his boots.

The music began when Colm brought out his fiddle. It was a battered instrument, the varnish worn away by his father’s thumbs years ago. Colm tucked it under his chin and played a jig, fast and frantic, the bow sawing across the strings. The rhythm pulled at the air, and slowly, people began to move.

Nora watched her brother. He was dancing with Aoife. It was a surprise to see them together, two people who had grown up sharing secrets and now shared a silence that felt heavy with unsaid things. Colm’s steps were quick, his eyes bright with a feverish energy that Nora recognized. It was the look he got when he talked about the shipyard, about the mainland, about a life that was not this one. But here, in the circle of light, he was smiling at Aoife, and she was smiling back, a genuine, unguarded smile that Nora hadn’t seen on her friend’s face since her husband died.

Nora felt a sudden, sharp pang in her chest. It was not jealousy, exactly, but a sense of displacement. She was the pillar that held the roof up, and pillars did not dance. Pillars did not smile. They stood, and they bore weight.

She turned away, seeking the comfort of the kitchen table. Father Ruane was there, tearing a piece of bread. He offered her a half.

“Your brother has the devil’s footwork,” the priest said, chewing slowly.

“He has his father’s impatience,” Nora said, taking the bread. “He wants to be everywhere at once.”

“And where is he now?”

“Here. For now.”

Ruane nodded, looking toward the dance. “Aoife is good for him. She grounds him. She knows the weight of things.”

Nora said nothing. She knew the weight of things better than anyone. She knew the weight of the coal bills, the weight of the salt sacks, the weight of the expectation that she would keep the Faherty name alive on this rock until she died.

The dance ended, and Colm laughed, breathless, wiping sweat from his forehead. He looked at Nora, and for a second, the defiance in his eyes softened into something pleading. He wanted her to join them. He wanted her to be part of the circle.

Nora shook her head slightly. She went to the fire, stirring the embers, needing the heat.

Then, a commotion near the back of the room drew everyone’s attention. Sean Breathnach had retrieved a fiddle from a case he had brought from the mainland. It was a new instrument, polished and gleaming, utterly out of place among the battered chairs and rough wool. He looked embarrassed, holding it like a dead bird.

“I… I learned a bit,” he said, his voice cracking. “In Dublin. Before I came out here.”

A ripple of laughter went through the room, not unkind, but amused. Peadar Kane, the boat captain, shouted, “Let’s hear it, then, Surveyor! Don’t leave us in suspense.”

Sean flushed. He looked at Aoife, who gave him an encouraging nod. He placed the bow on the strings and began to play.

It was a disaster. The notes were jagged, stumbling over each other. The rhythm was erratic, the tempo dragging and then rushing. It was a reel, or it was supposed to be, but it sounded more like a carpenter’s saw hitting a knot.

The laughter started small, a chuckle from old Mrs. Donnelly, and then grew. Peadar Kane slapped his knee. Even Father Ruane covered his mouth, his shoulders shaking.

Sean stopped, his face crimson. He looked ready to flee back to the window, to the cold dark outside.

“No, no,” Colm shouted, stepping forward. “Play it again! That was brilliant! It’s got character!”

“I’m not playing it again,” Sean said, though he didn’t move away.

“Please,” Aoife said, her voice cutting through the noise. “It’s honest. That’s what matters.”

Sean hesitated. He looked at Nora. She was still by the fire, holding her broom. He looked at her, waiting for her judgment. He had spent weeks watching her, measuring her life, and now he was playing a song for her audience.

Nora felt the room’s attention shift to her. She could stop it. She could tell him to pack it away, to be serious, to remember who they were. But she looked at his hands, trembling slightly, and she looked at the joy in the room, the rare, fragile joy that the island usually rationed like coal.

She tapped the broom handle against the floorboards. Once. Twice. A slow, steady beat.

Sean looked at her, surprised. Then he smiled, a tentative, grateful thing. He lifted the bow and began again.

He was still terrible. The notes still clashed. But Nora kept the beat, a rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* against the wood. Colm joined in on his fiddle, weaving a melody around Sean’s chaotic strumming, harmonizing where there was none. The room began to clap along. The awkwardness melted into something communal, something shared.

Nora felt the tension in her shoulders loosen, just a fraction. She watched Sean’s face. He wasn’t performing; he was participating. He was letting himself be mocked, letting himself be imperfect, and in doing so, he was becoming part of the island’s texture.

When the song ended, the applause was loud and genuine. Sean bowed, jokingly, and the laughter continued. He walked over to Nora, breathing heavily.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re a terrible musician,” Nora said, but there was no bite in her voice.

“I know,” he said. “But I’m trying.”

Nora looked at him. He was not like the other men from the mainland. He did not speak with the certainty of authority. He spoke with the hesitation of a man who knew he was asking for too much.

“Try harder,” she said. “It’s a long winter.”

He nodded, taking a mug of tea from Aoife’s tray. He sat down at the table near the fire, and the circle closed around him. He was no longer an outsider looking in; he was a man in the room.

Nora returned to the kitchen area. She checked the stove, ensuring the tea was still warm. Her mind drifted to the salt pans outside. They were cold now, banked with ash, waiting for the morning. She thought of the coal reserves, dwindling but sufficient, thanks to the storm’s delay and the rationing. She thought of Colm, dancing with Aoife, his eyes alive with a future he could barely see. She thought of Sean, stranded, learning the rhythm of the island one bad note at a time.

She felt a strange, quiet peace. It was not happiness, not exactly. It was the satisfaction of endurance. They were still here. They were still burning.

Father Ruane came over, holding two mugs. He handed one to her.

“You’re letting him get under your skin,” the priest said.

Nora sipped the tea. It was sweet, too sweet. “He’s useful. He helps at the pans.”

“He’s lonely,” Ruane corrected gently. “Like all of us.”

Nora looked at the priest. His face was lined, a map of the decades he had spent on this rock. He had buried most of the people who had taught him his prayers. He stayed because there was nowhere else to go, and because someone had to say the words when the light failed.

“I’m not lonely,” Nora said. “I have work.”

“Work is a good companion,” Ruane said. “But it’s a poor friend.”

Nora didn’t answer. She watched Aoife and Colm talking in the corner. Colm was leaning in close to Aoife, listening intently to something she was saying. His hand rested on the back of her chair. It was a small gesture, but it carried the weight of a promise.

Nora felt a flicker of fear. If Colm stayed, if he loved Aoife, would he still want to leave? Or would the island hold him, as it held her? She wanted him to stay. She wanted the Faherty name to remain. But she also wanted him to be happy, and she knew, with a cold clarity, that happiness on Inis Breac was a luxury they could rarely afford.

The ceilidh wore on. Songs were sung, stories were told, and the peat fire burned down to glowing embers. When it was time to leave, the wind had picked up again, whistling around the eaves of the schoolhouse.

Nora helped Aoife clean up, sweeping the crumbs into the dustpan. Sean came to help carry the chairs, stacking them neatly. He worked quietly, efficiently.

“Will you come to the pans tomorrow?” Nora asked him.

He paused, holding a chair. “If you’ll have me.”

“The coal is low,” she said. “We need to stretch it. I need to see if you can manage the bellows without stopping every five minutes.”

Sean smiled. “I’ll try not to stop.”

He left with the others, disappearing into the dark. Nora stood at the door, watching them go. The snow was beginning to fall again, soft and heavy.

Colm lingered inside. He stood by the fire, his fiddle in its case.

“Are you coming?” Nora asked.

“In a minute,” he said.

She waited. The silence stretched between them, filled only by the crackle of the embers.

“I like her,” Colm said finally. He didn’t look at Nora. He looked at the fire.

“I know,” Nora said.

“It’s not just that,” he said. “It’s… she listens. She doesn’t tell me I’m foolish for wanting to leave.”

Nora stiffened. “I don’t think you’re foolish.”

“You think I’m weak,” Colm said. He turned to face her. His eyes were red-rimmed. “You think I’m weak because I can’t bear it anymore.”

Nora looked at him. She saw the boy he had been, the boy who had watched their father die by the pans, the boy who had promised to help her keep them burning. And she saw the man he was becoming, a man who wanted more than survival.

“I think you’re strong,” Nora said softly. “Strong enough to know what you need.”

Colm looked at her, searching her face for a lie. He found none. He nodded, once, and walked to the door.

“Goodnight, Nora.”

“Goodnight, Colm.”

He left, and the door swung shut. Nora was alone in the schoolhouse. The fire was dying. She swept the last of the ash into the pan and carried it out to the bin. The cold hit her face, sharp and cleansing.

She looked toward the salt works. The building was dark, a black shape against the white snow. She walked toward it, her boots crunching on the frozen ground. She entered the boiling house and lit a lantern. The great iron pans stood empty, cold and heavy. She ran her hand along the edge of the nearest one. The iron was freezing.

She thought of Sean’s bad music. She thought of Colm’s dance. She thought of the forty-one souls who had started the year, and the thirty-two who remained.

She went to the coal store and checked the level. It was low, but there was enough. She banked the fire in the small stove for the night, ensuring it would hold until dawn.

Then she went home. The house was dark and cold. She lit the fire in the main room, the flames catching the kindling with a greedy hiss. She sat in her father’s chair and watched the fire burn.

For the first time in months, she did not count the hours. She did not calculate the cost. She just watched the light dance on the walls, and she let herself believe, just for a moment, that the island could hold them all.

## Chapter 7: The Edge of the World

The fog had lifted by mid-morning, leaving the island stripped of its softness. The rock of Inis Breac lay bare, grey and jagged against a sky that was the colour of bruised iron. The air tasted of salt and the lingering, metallic tang of the winter storms that had finally broken.

Nora stood at the edge of the quay, her hands tucked into the pockets of her wool coat. The *Mary Rose* was late. Peadar Kane’s boat had been due two days ago, but the tides and the government’s indecision had kept them waiting. Now, the engine’s cough echoed from behind the headland, a rhythmic thudding that vibrated in Nora’s chest.

Colm was beside her. He was pacing, a nervous habit he had picked up during the long weeks of the storm. He looked thinner than he had in autumn, the hollows of his cheeks more pronounced, his eyes too bright. He had not spoken of the shipyard since Christmas, but Nora knew the letter was in his coat pocket. She could see the bulge of the paper.

"He’s coming," Colm said. It was not a question.

"I see the wake," Nora said. Her voice was flat, stripped of inflection. "Don’t start your pacing. You’ll wear a groove in the stone."

Colm stopped, but his hands remained clenched at his sides. "Does it matter, Nora? If he recommends it? If the mainland takes us?"

"The decision is not his," Nora said, repeating the words she had told herself a hundred times since Sean Breathnach had first stepped onto the island. "He measures. He counts. He writes. The men in Dublin with the clean collars decide."

"And what do you want him to write?"

Nora looked away, toward the Skerry Head. The wind was picking up, whipping the sea spray against the black basalt. "I want the pans to keep boiling. I want the mill to grind. I want the people to stay."

"Those are different things," Colm said quietly.

The boat rounded the headland. It was a small vessel, painted white with peeling green trim, riding low in the water. Peadar Kane stood at the wheel, his face obscured by the shadow of the cabin. Behind him, Sean Breathnach stood at the rail, holding onto the rigging with one hand. He wore his surveyor’s coat, buttoned to the throat, and his hat was pulled low. He looked tired. The isolation had changed him, too. The crisp lines of the mainland official had softened, worn down by the wind and the wait.

The boat nudged against the fenders. A rope was thrown. Colm moved to catch it, his movements efficient, practiced. Nora remained where she was. She watched Sean step onto the wet wood of the quay. He did not look at her immediately. He looked at the island, scanning the rooftops, the chimneys, the small gardens clinging to the soil behind the stone walls. He was checking his inventory.

Peadar jumped down, tying the line to a cleat. "Quiet sea today, Miss Faherty. Better than last month."

"Thank you, Peadar," Nora said.

Sean approached them. He removed his hat, holding it in both hands. He looked at Nora, then at Colm. His eyes were dark, shadowed by sleepless nights. "Miss Faherty. Mr. Faherty."

"Mr. Breathnach," Nora said. She did not offer her hand. "You are leaving."

"I am."

"Will you be returning?"

Sean hesitated. The wind gusted, tearing a loose strand of hair from Nora’s bun. She did not reach to fix it. "The report is done," he said. "The government will review it. If they require further data, I will return. If they decide to withdraw support... I will not."

"And if they keep the island?"

"Then I will return," Sean said. "There is much to survey. The cliff faces are shifting. The mill foundation needs reinforcement."

Nora studied him. He was no longer an outsider. He had eaten their food, worked beside them in the boiling house, played a fiddle that sounded like a cat being strangled. He had seen the fear in their eyes when the coal ran out. He had seen the dignity with which they bore it.

"You should go to Aoife," Nora said. "She will have made tea. And Peadar will want to unload the mail and the new supplies."

Sean nodded. He looked at Colm. "I have a letter for you, Colm. From the shipyard. Peadar brought it."

Colm stiffened. "I have one already."

"This one is from the foreman. It says your offer stands. Until March."

Colm looked at Nora. The silence between the three of them was heavy, filled with the unspoken weight of choice.

"Go to Aoife," Nora said again, her voice harder now. "Take the letter. Read it later. Or burn it. But go."

Sean looked at her, searching for something in her face. He found only the mask she had perfected over the last decade. He bowed his head slightly and walked past them toward the path that led to the schoolhouse. Peadar followed him, carrying a crate of tinned goods.

Colm watched him go. Then he turned to Nora. "I’m going to help unload."

"Don’t," Nora said. "Go to the pans. The brine is ready. I need the temperature checked."

Colm hesitated, then nodded. He walked away, his shoulders hunched against the wind. Nora watched him go until he disappeared around the corner of the boiling house. Then she turned and walked toward Skerry Head.

She did not know why she went. It was a habit, perhaps. A way to measure the time before the next crisis. The path was steep and slippery with moss. She climbed until her lungs burned, until the wind was a solid wall against her chest.

At the top, the view opened up. The sea stretched to the horizon, endless and indifferent. The Skerry Head jutted out into the water, a finger of rock pointing to the mainland. At its tip stood the shell of the old mill, roofless since the fire of 1931, its sails long since taken by the sea. She could not see the mainland today. The haze was too thick.

She sat on a flat stone, her back against the wind. She pulled her coat tighter. She thought of her father, standing here years ago, pointing out the safe channels for the boats. She thought of the forty-one people who lived below her. Forty-one. It was a number that had shrunk every year. First the young men, then the old, then the children. Now it was just the stubborn, the broken, and the ones who had nowhere else to go.

She heard footsteps. She did not turn.

Sean Breathnach climbed the last few yards, breathing heavily. He stopped a few feet away, holding onto the stone for balance. He looked out at the sea, not at her.

"I did not mean to be cruel," he said. His voice was lost in the wind, so she waited until it died down before he spoke again. "About the letter."

Nora kept her eyes on the horizon. "You are not cruel, Mr. Breathnach. You are efficient."

"I am a surveyor," he said. "We are trained to see what is there, not what we wish to be there."

"And what is there?"

He moved closer, sitting on the stone beside her. He did not touch her. There was a distance between them, physical and emotional, that had been maintained for months.

"The island is dying," Sean said. "Not today. Not tomorrow. But the statistics are clear. The population is aging. The salt trade is declining due to cheaper industrial production on the mainland. The mill is inefficient. The roads are non-existent. The school has one teacher for eight children."

"And the people?" Nora asked. "Are they a statistic?"

"The people are the problem," Sean said, and then he paused, seeing the sharp look she gave him. "No. That is not what I mean. The people are the reason the island remains. But they are also the reason it cannot be saved in its current form. They are too proud to accept charity. Too stubborn to leave. And too poor to invest in the improvements that would make it viable."

Nora looked at him. "And your report?"

Sean looked at his hands. They were rougher than they had been in October. There was dirt under his fingernails. "I recommended support. Subsidies for the salt works. Grants for the mill. A new boat. Not evacuation."

Nora felt a knot in her chest loosen, just slightly. "Why?"

"Because I saw you," Sean said. He looked at her, and for the first time, he did not look away. "I saw you in the boiling house. I saw you choose to freeze the schoolhouse rather than let the salt fail. I saw Colm dancing, despite everything. I saw Aoife teaching the children when she was exhausted. This is not a community that can be moved. It is a community that must be held together. If you evacuate them, you destroy something that cannot be replicated. You scatter them, and they disappear. Here, they are Inis Breac. There, they are just the Irish poor."

Nora looked back at the sea. The wind was stronger now, carrying the scent of rain. "And if the government decides to cut the subsidies anyway?"

"Then I will be wrong," Sean said. "And I will have to live with that. But I cannot lie. I cannot write that this place is worthless. It is not."

Nora stood up. Her legs were numb from the cold. "You should go. The boat leaves in an hour. You have your report."

Sean stood as well. He put his hat back on. "Will you be here? Next year?"

"I am always here," Nora said. "Where else would I go?"

Sean nodded. He reached into his coat pocket and pulled out a small object. He placed it on the stone between them. It was a compass. Brass, tarnished, with a cracked glass face.

"For the fog," he said. "It is more accurate than the stars."

Nora looked at the compass. She did not pick it up. "I know the way home, Mr. Breathnach. I have known it all my life."

Sean smiled, a small, sad thing. "Goodbye, Nora."

He turned and walked down the path. Nora watched him go. She did not call out to him. She stood there until he was a speck against the grey stone, then a shadow, then nothing.

She looked down at the compass. It lay on the lichen-covered rock, a small circle of brass against the green. She picked it up. It was cold. She put it in her pocket.

She walked back to the village. The sun had broken through the clouds, casting long, sharp shadows across the slate roofs. Smoke rose from the chimneys, thin and grey. She could hear the children’s voices from the schoolhouse, singing something cheerful, out of tune.

She passed the school. Aoife was outside, hanging laundry on a line. She looked pale, but she was smiling. She saw Nora and waved.

"He is gone?" Aoife asked.

"He is leaving," Nora said.

Aoife nodded. She smoothed a sheet over the line. "He is a good man, Nora. In a different life, he might have been happy here."

Nora said nothing. She walked past her, up the hill toward the boiling house.

Colm was waiting for her at the door. He was holding a piece of paper. His face was unreadable.

Nora stopped. "What is it?"

Colm crumpled the paper in his hand. "I went to the shipyard office in my mind," he said. "I imagined walking in. Signing the papers. Leaving."

"And?"

"And I could not do it." Colm looked at her, his eyes red-rimmed. "I cannot leave you, Nora. Not yet. The island needs you. And I..." He paused. "I am not ready."

Nora felt a surge of anger, then it faded, replaced by a weary relief. "You are a fool, Colm."

"I know."

"The pans are boiling," Nora said. "We need more peat. The coal is low."

Colm nodded. He threw the crumpled letter into the fire pit outside the house. It caught quickly, turning to ash.

"I will get the peat," he said.

Nora watched him walk away. She looked at the compass in her pocket. She took it out, holding it in her palm. The needle swung slightly, then settled, pointing north. Toward the mainland. Toward the world that had forgotten them.

She put the compass back. She turned and entered the boiling house. The heat hit her face, thick and humid, smelling of salt and smoke. The large iron pans bubbled vigorously, the brine thickening. She picked up the paddle. She began to stir.

Outside, the wind howled, but inside, the rhythm continued. The scrape of the paddle against the iron. The crackle of the fire. The steady, unyielding pulse of the island, refusing to stop.

## Chapter 8: The Weight of Paper

The fog had lifted by noon, leaving the island scrubbed and grey. It was March, the month when the light returned in thin, watery slivers that did little to warm the stone. Nora stood at the edge of the drying beds, her hands tucked into the pockets of her wool coat, watching the sea churn against the black basalt of Skerry Head. The wind here was constant, a physical pressure against the chest, carrying the scent of brine and rotting kelp. Below her, the boiling pans sat cold and empty, the iron lids rusted shut, waiting for the next load of coal that Peadar Kane had promised would come by Friday.

It had been a hard winter. The storms had lasted longer than any living memory could recall, turning the sea into a wall of white fury that kept the supply boat anchored in the mainland harbor for twenty-two days. They had burned furniture in the schoolhouse and shared blankets in the church nave, Father Ruane presiding over their shivering congregation with a stoicism that bordered on indifference. But they had survived. The forty-one souls of Inis Breac remained, diminished in number but intact in spirit, or so Nora told herself.

She turned from the cliff edge and walked back toward the salt works. The buildings were low and squat, built into the wind’s shelter, their roofs weighted down with heavy stones to prevent them from being peeled off by the gales. Inside the boiling house, the silence was heavy. The fire pits were dark, the air thick with the lingering smell of peat smoke and old salt. Colm was already there, sweeping the floor with a bundle of twigs. He moved with a new energy, a purposefulness that had been absent during the long months of waiting for Sean Breathnach’s report.

"You’re up early," Nora said, closing the heavy oak door behind her. The latch clicked, sealing them in.

Colm didn’t look up. He continued sweeping, the bristles rasping against the grit. "Couldn’t sleep. Kept thinking about the shipyard. The foreman said if I wasn’t back by April, he’d give my spot to a man from Galway."

Nora leaned against the workbench, crossing her arms. "And what did you tell him?"

"I told him I was thinking." Colm stopped sweeping and leaned on the broom handle, looking at her. His face was gaunt, the skin around his eyes bruised with fatigue, but his gaze was steady. "I told him I needed until the next tide."

Nora felt a tightening in her throat. For months, this had been the unspoken shadow between them. Colm’s restlessness had been a constant irritant, a reminder that the island was not a home but a holding cell. He had watched the horizon with the hunger of a prisoner watching the gates. When Sean Breathnach had arrived, Colm had seen a lifeline, a way out through the bureaucracy of the mainland. When Sean left, taking his surveyor’s bag and his quiet, assessing questions with him, Colm had seemed to deflate, resigned to the fate of the rock. But now, with the threat of evacuation still hanging over them like a guillotine, Colm’s decision to stay felt less like loyalty and more like a trap.

"Is that all?" Nora asked. "Thinking?"

"I’m staying, Nora," Colm said. "For one more year. Just one. If the pans don’t pay enough, if the mill fails, if the boat stops coming... then I go. But not yet. Not while there’s still salt to boil."

Nora looked away, staring at the cold iron of the pans. She wanted to argue. She wanted to tell him that one more year was just another way of dying slowly. She wanted to remind him of the shipyard, of the wages, of the life he had described in such vivid detail—electricity, paved roads, a woman who didn’t know his grandfather’s name. But she remembered the ceilidh at Christmas. She remembered Colm dancing with Aoife Mullan, the way his hand had rested on her waist with a tenderness she had never seen him show anyone else. Aoife, widowed and weary, had laughed—a genuine, bright sound that had startled the room into silence for a heartbeat before the fiddle resumed. Colm had looked at her as if she were the only fixed point in a spinning world.

"Fine," Nora said, the word tasting like ash. "But you’ll help me with the coal storage. Peadar is bringing two tons on Friday. We’ll need to move it all before the tide turns."

Colm nodded, a small, tight smile touching his lips. "I’ll be here."

***

The letter arrived three days later, tucked inside the crate of coal and flour that Peadar Kane unloaded with his usual gruff efficiency. The captain didn’t stay for tea. He checked the weather, muttered something about a coming squall, and pushed off before Nora could even offer him a cup. He was a man of tides and timing, and his patience for human sentiment was as limited as the island’s resources.

Nora carried the crate into the kitchen, her arms aching from the weight of the sacks. She set it on the table, pushing aside the bowl of boiled potatoes and the jar of pickled onions. The letter was addressed to her, in Sean Breathnach’s neat, angular handwriting. The ink was blue, precise and impersonal.

She broke the seal. The paper was expensive, thicker than the stationery Father Ruane used for his parish records. She sat down at the table, the wood cold beneath her palms, and read.

*Dear Ms. Faherty,*

*Following my assessment of Inis Breac in the autumn of 1952 and my observations during the winter months, I have submitted my final report to the Department of Local Government. The conditions on the island are, as you know, harsh. The infrastructure is deteriorating, the population is declining, and the economic viability of the salt works and the grain mill is marginal at best.*

*However, the human element of this assessment cannot be ignored. The resilience of the community, the maintenance of the boiling houses, and the continued operation of the school under difficult circumstances suggest a social cohesion that is rare in modern rural Ireland. To evacuate the island at this time would not only displace forty-one citizens but also extinguish a cultural and economic tradition that has persisted for centuries.*

*Therefore, I have recommended that the island be supported rather than evacuated. I have proposed a grant for the repair of the Skerry Head mill and a subsidy for the coal supply to ensure the continuity of the salt works. Please note that this recommendation is advisory only. The final decision rests with the Minister, and it may be influenced by factors beyond my control, including budgetary constraints and political pressures from the mainland.*

*You should prepare for the possibility that my advice may be overruled. But for now, the pans must be fired.*

*Yours sincerely,*

*Sean Breathnach*
*Surveyor, Third Class*

Nora read the letter twice. Then a third time. She folded it carefully, along the creases, and placed it inside the pocket of her apron. She felt no surge of joy, no wave of relief. There was only a heavy, settling stone in her stomach. *Advisory only.* The words mocked her. They had survived the winter, they had survived the storms, and now they were to be judged by men in offices in Dublin who had never felt the wind cut through their wool coats.

She stood up and walked to the window. Outside, the sky was a pale, washed-out blue. The sea was calmer today, the swells long and rolling. She could see Colm in the distance, near the mill, carrying a sack of grain. He moved with a steady rhythm, his back straight. He had chosen to stay. For now.

***

The next morning, Nora woke before dawn. The house was silent, the air still cold enough to see her breath. She dressed in the dark, pulling on her thick socks, her wool stockings, and her heavy boots. She tied her kerchief tight around her head, tucking away any stray strands of hair that the wind might pull loose.

She stepped out into the yard. The sky was beginning to lighten in the east, a faint smear of grey against the black. The world was quiet, save for the distant crash of the surf and the caw of a single jackdaw circling the chimney stacks.

Nora walked to the boiling house. The door groaned as she pushed it open. The smell of iron and damp stone greeted her, familiar and comforting. She moved through the shadows, her hands knowing the path by heart. She checked the flues, ensuring they were clear of debris. She checked the coal hopper, noting the level of the black lumps that Peadar had delivered.

Then, she went to the firebox. She took a match from her pocket, struck it against the rough stone wall, and lit the kindling. The flame caught, small and orange, trembling in the draft. She fed it with strips of dry wood, then with larger pieces of peat. The smoke began to rise, curling up toward the chimney.

She lit the second pan. Then the third. By the time the sun began to crest the horizon, painting the sea in shades of copper and blood, all four pans were roaring. The water in the brine vats began to steam. The heat radiated outward, pushing back the chill of the March morning.

Colm appeared at the doorway. He was wearing his work clothes, his face smudged with soot from the mill. He looked tired, but his eyes were clear. He didn’t speak. He simply walked over to the nearest pan, picked up the long-handled skimmer, and began to work, lifting the impurities from the surface of the boiling brine.

Nora watched him for a moment. She saw the strain in his shoulders, the tension in his jaw. He was staying, but he was not happy. He was bound by duty, by love for Aoife, perhaps, or by a fear of the unknown mainland. Nora understood that fear. She had lived with it for ten years, since her father’s death, when she had stepped into the boiling house and taken the skimmer from his cold hands.

She joined him. She took the skimmer from his hand, her fingers brushing against his. He let go, stepping back to let her take the lead. It was a small gesture, but it carried the weight of a thousand unspoken conversations.

The brine bubbled, thick and white. The salt began to form at the edges, crystallizing in the heat. Nora watched it grow, minute by minute, a slow accumulation of substance from the void of the sea. It was hard work, repetitive and exhausting, but it was real. It was the only thing that mattered. The letter was paper. The report was words. The salt was solid. It could be eaten. It could be sold. It could keep them alive.

Father Ruane arrived shortly after, walking up the path from the chapel with his head bowed against the wind. He wore his black cassock, the hem dragging in the mud. He stopped at the doorway, watching Nora and Colm work.

"The pans are hot," he said, his voice raspy.

"They are," Nora said, not looking up. She skimmed a layer of foam from the pan.

"Sean Breathnach’s letter?" the priest asked.

Nora paused. She wiped her hands on her apron and turned to face him. "It says we are supported. For now."

Father Ruane nodded slowly. He walked into the building, the heat enveloping him. He looked around at the steaming pans, at the soot-stained walls, at the two siblings working in silence. "Supported," he murmured. "A fragile thing, support. Like a house of cards in a wind tunnel."

"We’re not made of cards, Father," Nora said.

"No," he agreed, a faint smile touching his lips. "You’re made of salt and iron. Harder to break, but harder to bend."

He left them to their work. Nora turned back to the pan. The sun was fully up now, bright and harsh, reflecting off the white crust of salt forming on the bottom of the vat. She dipped the skimmer in, lifting the crystals. They sparkled in the light, pure and sharp.

Colm moved to the next pan, picking up his own skimmer. He didn’t speak, but he worked beside her, his movements synchronized with hers. The rhythm of the boiling house returned, the clank of iron, the hiss of steam, the scrape of the skimmer. It was the sound of their survival.

Nora looked out the doorway. The sea stretched to the horizon, endless and indifferent. Somewhere out there, beyond the grey line of the water, was the mainland. It was a place of noise and money and forgetting. She felt a pang of envy for Colm’s ability to imagine it, to want it. But then she looked at the salt in the pan, growing thicker, heavier. This was her life. It was hard, it was lonely, and it was fragile. But it was hers.

She dipped the skimmer again. The salt came up, white and bright. She dumped it into the wooden trough, where it would dry and be bagged. The work continued. The day began. And on Inis Breac, as it had for generations, the pans boiled, burning fuel and flesh to pull the sweetness from the sea.
