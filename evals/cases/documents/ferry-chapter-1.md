# Chapter One: The Crossing

Dana Okafor took the first crossing of the day at six-fifteen, the way she
had every weekday since she was twenty-two. The *Marigold* was forty feet of
steel and diesel and peeling white paint, licensed for twelve passengers and
two cars, and in October she rarely carried more than four of either.

This morning it was Mr. Halvorsen with his milk cans, the two Deluca boys on
their way to the school in Port Ash, and a woman Dana did not know, who stood
at the rail in a good wool coat with a small suitcase between her feet.

Dana backed the ferry off the Calloway slip, swung her bow toward the far
shore, and opened the throttle. The engine settled into its low, familiar
shudder. Through the wheelhouse glass the town fell away behind them: the
church spire, the feed store, the row of clapboard houses along Water Street,
and at the end of the row the yellow house where she and Mara had grown up.

The lake was a vast, shimmering, glimmering mirror of silvery liquid glass
that stretched endlessly, eternally, infinitely toward the distant, faraway
horizon, where the beautiful golden rays of the magnificent rising sun kissed
the gentle, rippling, sparkling surface of the calm and peaceful waters with
a tender, loving, radiant glow of pure and perfect morning light.

Halfway across, the woman in the wool coat knocked on the wheelhouse door.

"You're Dana," she said. It was not a question. "Mara's sister."

Dana kept one hand on the wheel. "Who's asking?"

"Ruth Abernathy. I knew Mara in the city. We shared a flat for a while, after
she first moved there." The woman's eyes went to the scar across the back of
Dana's left hand, the old rope burn, white and shiny, and came back to her
face. "She talked about you. About this boat."

Mara had left Calloway three springs ago, on this same crossing, with two
duffel bags and a job offer from a design firm she would not name. She had
sent a postcard that first summer and another at Christmas, and then nothing.
Dana had stopped checking the post office box in the second year. She had not
stopped looking up at the yellow house every morning as the ferry pulled
away.

"Is she all right?" Dana asked.

Ruth Abernathy looked out at the water for a long moment before she answered.
"I was hoping you could tell me," she said. "She left the flat in August. She
left most of her things. I thought she might have come home."

"She hasn't."

"No," Ruth said. "I can see that."

They did not speak again until the far slip. Dana brought the *Marigold* in
against the pilings with barely a bump, the way her father had taught her,
and the Deluca boys ran for the school bus and Mr. Halvorsen rolled his cans
up the ramp. Ruth Abernathy picked up her suitcase and stood for a moment at
the top of the ramp.

"I'm staying at the inn in Port Ash," she said. "Until Friday. If you hear
anything."

Dana watched her walk up the hill. Then she went back into the wheelhouse
and sat down on the stool and looked at her hands on the wheel, the scarred
one and the other, until the first passengers for the return crossing began
to gather on the slip.

On the way back she did not look at the yellow house. It was the first
morning in three years that she had not.
