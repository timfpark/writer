# The Escapement

At quarter to six the market stalls outside were coming down, canvas slapping
as the iron poles came loose, and Solange Duret was sweeping brass shavings
into a folded card when the bell above the door rang.

She did not look up from the bench until the footsteps stopped at the glass
counter. Her uncle Henri stood there in a wet trench coat, holding a small
chamois pouch between both hands as if it were still warm.

"We close at six," Solange said.

"I saw the sign on the glass. I walked past twice before I came in." He set
the pouch on the counter and unknotted the drawstring with stiff fingers.
Inside lay a silver hunter-case pocket watch, the bow bent slightly left and
the lid dull with six years of drawer tarnish.

She knew it before he opened the lid. Her father had worn it on a braided
leather fob until the spring went in his last autumn, and Henri had taken it
off the bedside table the morning after the service without asking anyone.

"It stopped in November," Henri said. "The jeweler on Rue Saint-Clair told me
the hairspring was kinked and he didn't work on lever movements anymore. He
gave me your card."

Solange pulled her loupe down from her forehead, opened the case back with
her thumbnail, and angled the movement under the bench lamp. The hairspring
was fine. The balance staff had taken a hard drop, and someone had tried to
free the train with heavy sewing oil that had gummed into amber varnish
around the jewels.

"You dropped it," she said.

"On the kitchen tile. Last winter."

"And then you oiled it."

"I thought it was just dry." He shifted his weight from one shoe to the
other. "Solange, I came because I've felt guilty ever since the funeral and I
want us to reconcile. I know I handled your father's estate poorly, and I
have carried a lot of regret about shutting you out of the workshop decisions
when you were the one who actually loved the trade."

Solange set the watch down on the green mat. "You stayed away for six years
because you couldn't face what you did to my father's shop," she said. "You
sold his lathe and his spare movements to a dealer in Lyon without telling me
because you wanted the money quickly, and now you're angry with yourself, so
you brought his watch here hoping a repair bill will settle everything."

Henri looked down at the counter. The streetlamp outside flickered on,
throwing a pale rectangle across the brass clocks on the wall, where thirty
pendulums ticked slightly out of phase.

"Can it be fixed?" he asked quietly.

"It needs a new staff and a full strip in the ultrasonic," Solange said.
"Three weeks. Ninety francs up front for parts."

He reached into his coat pocket for his wallet, counted out the bills onto
the glass, and paused with his hand still on the leather. "I didn't come only
for the watch."

"I know," Solange said. She wrote out a claim ticket in neat block letters,
tore it along the perforation, and slid it across the glass beside the pouch.
"The ticket has my hours on the back. Don't come on Tuesdays; I'm at the
clock tower."

He folded the slip into his breast pocket, touched the brim of his hat, and
went out into the rain. Solange waited until the bell stopped swinging, then
slipped the watch under a glass dome beside her lamp.
