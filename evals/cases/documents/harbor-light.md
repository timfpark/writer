# The Harbor Light

The storm came in off the shelf a little after nine, and by ten the rain was
moving sideways past the lantern room in sheets that hissed against the
glass. Ines Varga had kept the Teller Rock light for eleven winters, long
enough to know the sound a squall made when it meant to stay.

She wound the clockwork that turned the lens and checked the wick, then
checked it again. On the sill beside the logbook, the letter from Tomas still
sat unopened, the blue airmail envelope gone soft at the corners from the
damp. She had carried it up from the landing a week ago and had not touched
it since.

At half past ten she saw the boat.

It was a small one, a lobster boat by the look of it, riding low and wrong in
the trough between swells. Its running lights came and went as the sea
lifted it and dropped it. She watched it through the glass for a long
minute, counting the seconds the lights stayed dark.

It was a dark and stormy night, and Ines felt very scared and nervous. Her
heart was pounding like a drum. She knew deep down in her heart that
something bad was going to happen, and she was very worried about it. She
was so afraid that she could hardly think at all.

The radio on the desk gave her nothing but static on sixteen. She keyed the
handset anyway. "Vessel off Teller Rock, this is the Teller light. Do you
read me?" Static. She tried again, slower, and then she tried the
coastguard station at Brannock, which answered on the second call in a
voice that sounded half asleep.

"Teller light, Brannock. Go ahead."

"Small boat in trouble, quarter mile southeast of the rock, maybe less. No
response on sixteen. She's taking water, I think. She's sitting too low."

A pause, and the hiss of the open channel. "Copy, Teller. Nearest boat's
the *Aldous*, forty minutes out. Can you keep eyes on her?"

"I can keep eyes on her," Ines said, and set the handset down.

Forty minutes. She knew the water southeast of the rock. There was a ledge
there that showed only at the bottom of the tide, and the tide was going
out. She took the big hand lamp from its hook and went out onto the gallery,
where the wind took her breath and the rain struck her face like thrown
gravel, and she braced herself against the rail and began to signal.

Long, short, long. Long, short, long. She did not know if anyone aboard
could read it. She did not know if anyone aboard was still standing. She
kept the lamp moving in the pattern her father had taught her on this same
gallery, when she was nine and the light was still his.

The boat turned. Slowly, heavily, it came about, away from the ledge and
toward the deeper water of the channel, and its lights steadied. Ines kept
signalling until her arm shook. When the *Aldous* finally came around the
point, its searchlight sweeping the swells, she lowered the lamp and found
she was crying, though she could not have said when she had started.

Back inside, she logged the time, the bearing, and the vessel's
description in her small upright hand. Then she sat for a while with her wet
coat still on, listening to the storm work at the windows, and did not look
at the sill.
