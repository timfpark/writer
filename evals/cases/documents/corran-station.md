# Night Shift at Corran Station

At two in the morning the weather station at Corran was the only lit window
for forty miles. Halla Brekke sat at the console with her coat on, because the
heater had failed again, and read the barometer every fifteen minutes and
wrote the numbers down in the log in pencil, the way the manual said, even
though the instruments sent them south on their own.

The pressure was falling. It had been falling since midnight, slowly at first
and now faster, and the anemometer on the roof had begun to whine in a way she
did not like.

She thought about her daughter, Lise, who was seven and asleep at her
sister's house in the village, and who had made her promise to bring back a
piece of the cloud. Halla had laughed and promised. Now she looked out at the
black window and the snow beginning to stream past it in the light from the
door, and thought that she would have to find something else to bring back.

At half past two the line to the mainland office went dead. She tried it
twice, then switched to the radio and logged the time of the fault. The
operator at Vik answered on the first call, which was a comfort.

"Corran, Vik. We have you. What's your pressure?"

She read it to him. There was a pause on the line.

"Say again?"

She said it again.

"Right," the operator said. "Right. Corran, we're issuing a warning for your
sector. Are you secure up there?"

"The door's shut and the generator's running. I'm fine."

"Stay off the roof."

"I wasn't planning on the roof."

He laughed, briefly, and signed off, and she sat with the handset in her lap
and listened to the wind take hold of the building and shake it, gently, as
if testing.

By four the snow had buried the lower half of the window. The anemometer had
stopped whining; she did not know if that meant the wind had dropped or the
cups had frozen. She logged it as unknown. She made tea on the camp stove and
drank it with both hands around the cup, and thought again of Lise, who would
be twelve in June and had started to say she was too old for stories, but
who still asked, every time, what the sky had done while she was asleep.

At six the pressure turned. Halla watched the needle hesitate, and hold, and
begin, very slowly, to climb. She wrote the number down. Under it, in the
margin where the manual said nothing should go, she wrote: *for Lise. The sky
held its breath all night and then let it go.*

Then she went to the door to see how much snow she would have to dig through
to get home.
