# Grafting

Every March, on the first dry morning after the frost broke, Wen Ruolan
carried her grafting knife and a coffee tin of wax down to the bottom row of
the orchard, and every March since he was six her grandson Li had carried the
scions for her, wrapped in damp newspaper like something that might bruise.

He was fourteen now and taller than she was, and he carried them without
being asked.

The rootstock trees stood in a crooked line along the fence, each cut back to
a stub the height of her knee. She chose the first one, ran her thumb along
the cut bark to find the green beneath, and held out her hand. Li unwrapped a
scion, a pencil-thin length of last year's wood from the old Northern Spy
by the house, and gave it to her butt-end first, the way she had taught him.

"Watch the angle," she said. She drew the knife toward herself in one long
stroke and the wood came away in a clean, pale wedge. "Long and shallow.
More surface, more chance."

"You say that every year."

"Every year it's still true."

She made the matching cut in the rootstock, fitted the two together until
the thin green lines beneath the bark met, and bound them tight with a strip
of rubber. Li opened the tin and she painted the join with wax, thick, so
that no air would reach the wound. The whole of it took less than two
minutes. It had taken her most of a summer, the year she was fourteen, to
learn to do it at all.

They worked down the row. The sun came over the ridge and burned the mist
off the grass, and the smell of the wax, warm and resinous, rose around them.
At the seventh tree Li asked if he could make the cut.

She looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised her, though she
supposed it should not have. She gave him the knife.

His first cut was too steep. He saw it before she said anything and made it
again, and the second was long and shallow and clean. She fitted the scion
herself and let him bind it, and when he had painted the wax she pressed her
thumb against the join to feel that it held.

"Will it take?" he asked.

"We'll know in May." She wiped the blade on her apron. "If the buds break,
it took. If they don't, we cut it back and try again next year. The tree
doesn't mind. It has time."

They finished the row by eleven. Walking back up toward the house, Li carried
the empty tin and the folded newspaper, and Wen carried the knife, closed,
in her apron pocket, where it had been for forty years. At the top of the
slope she stopped to look back at the line of stubs with their pale caps of
wax, and Li stopped beside her without asking why.

In May, nine of the ten grafts broke bud. The one that did not was his.

He was out in the rain the next morning, before she was awake, cutting it
back to try again.
