# Chapter Two: The Wheelhouse

The next morning Dana was at the slip before the first light of dawn, an hour
earlier than she needed to be. She had not slept. She let herself into the
wheelhouse with the key on her ring and stood in the dark, breathing the
smell of diesel and old coffee, waiting for her eyes to adjust.

She had decided, somewhere around three in the morning, to go through the
wheelhouse properly. Mara had worked the ferry with her for two summers
before she left, and Mara had always hidden things: notes, sweets, once a
pair of earrings she was not allowed to wear. If her sister had come back,
even for an hour, even in secret, she would have come here.

Dana started with the chart drawer. Tide tables, a broken pair of dividers,
a stack of crossing logs held together with a bulldog clip. Under the logs,
pushed to the very back, she found a brass key she had never seen before. It
was old and heavy, with a paper tag tied to the bow, and on the tag someone
had written a single word in pencil: *Lindqvist*.

She turned it over in her fingers. She did not know anyone called Lindqvist.
She put the key in her coat pocket and kept looking.

The cabinet under the radio held flares, a first aid kit, and a coil of spare
line. The bench seat lifted to show life jackets that needed replacing. Behind
the life jackets, wedged against the hull, there was a folded sheet of the
cheap lined paper they kept for the crossing logs.

Dana sat down on the bench and unfolded it. The afternoon sun slanted through
the wheelhouse windows and lay across the page in a long gold bar, and she had
to tilt the paper away from it to read.

It was Mara's handwriting. There was no date.

*D. I'm sorry about the boat. I'm sorry about all of it. Don't come looking.
I'll be all right. I always am. M.*

Dana read it three times. Then she folded it again along its old creases and
held it in both hands.

Sorry about the boat. She did not know what that meant. Nothing had happened
to the boat. The *Marigold* had run every crossing, every weekday, for as long
as she could remember.

She thought of Ruth Abernathy in the good wool coat, saying *she left most of
her things*. She thought of the postcards, the first one showing a skyline
she did not recognize, the second a painting of a winter street. Mara, who
had never once left Calloway, who had cried on her first night at summer camp
twenty miles up the shore, writing postcards from a city Dana had never seen.

At six the first passengers began to arrive on the slip. Dana put the note in
her pocket with the key, started the engine, and went out to drop the ramp.
Mr. Halvorsen was there with his milk cans, and he nodded to her the way he
always did, and she nodded back.

All the way across she kept her hand in her pocket, closed around the brass
key, feeling the shape of the paper tag against her palm.

When she came back to the Calloway slip, she called the inn in Port Ash from
the pay phone outside the feed store and asked for Ruth Abernathy.

"She checked out," the clerk said. "Last night. Didn't leave a forwarding
address."
